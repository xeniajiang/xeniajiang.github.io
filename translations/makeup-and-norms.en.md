# I Want to Wear Makeup, but Wanting It Is Not “Progressive”

## Why Desire Turns Toward Norms

“Rejecting makeup,” or “aesthetic freedom”? This question has long been a point of contention in public feminist debate.

In everyday life, many people have felt a longing to wear makeup and dress themselves up, and have sometimes found that longing confusing. As feminists, we have encountered extensive discussions of how beauty labor and visual capitalism alienate women. Yet such critiques do not directly eliminate the desire to “become beautiful.” We may understand perfectly well that prevailing definitions of beauty come from gendered norms and still experience pleasure, excitement, and passion through them.

I call this kind of desire **norm-oriented desire**: desire directed toward normative images, roles, and forms of life.

It appears paradoxical. Rather than expanding freedom, it turns explicitly toward cultural symbols and bodily images already appropriated and organized by normative culture. When norm-oriented desire becomes a concrete decision and enters the everyday practices through which we adorn our bodies, are we asserting bodily autonomy, or allowing cultural norms to inscribe themselves on our bodies? Are we becoming freer, or more thoroughly disciplined?

At the most fundamental level, why does norm-oriented desire exist?

How should we live with such desires in ourselves, and how should we relate to others who have them?

## Andrea Long Chu: Desire Cannot Be Mobilized in the Name of “Progress”

Some strands of radical feminism argue that prevailing forms of sexuality are organized around men. From this perspective, even heterosexual women should free themselves from what these feminists regard as “bad sex,” stop dating men or forming intimate relationships with them, and build relationships with women instead.

The concept of “false consciousness” is often used to explain women’s norm-oriented desires, including the desire to dress up or wear makeup. Such desires are said to result from the internalization of patriarchal ideas and are therefore treated as false forms of consciousness or mistaken desires. This approach denies that these desires possess any legitimate status in themselves. Feminists are then expected to use reason to discipline and politically redirect desire—to “mobilize” it toward progressive ends.

The language of false consciousness dismisses bodily desire too quickly. It also assumes that desire can be manipulated at will. In a series of essays, most notably “On Liking Women,” the trans writer and critic Andrea Long Chu makes a clear argument: desire cannot simply be mobilized for political purposes.

In Chu’s account, transition is bound up not with an abstract or academic notion of “gender authenticity,” but with desires that are mundane, worldly, and not especially “progressive”: lipstick, mascara, dresses, breasts, crying freely at the movies, becoming someone’s girlfriend, receiving a tender and ambiguous form of gendered preference, sharing intimacy with female friends, and feeling sexy and desired.

This is precisely the problem: desire does not obey rules about what we are supposed to want.

Chu deliberately places her desires where they are most vulnerable to attack: shaped eyebrows, filled-in lip lines, dresses, breasts, feminized bodies, and gendered fantasies of relationships. In her writing, desire repeatedly adheres to symbols that are vulgar, outdated, and already criticized countless times. A person may understand perfectly well that a desire comes from a gender order and still be genuinely drawn by it, unable to separate it from their embodied experience.

## Hume: Belief and Desire Can Come Apart

Andrea Long Chu reveals the limits of progressive attempts to mobilize desire. A Humean account of psychology can further explain why these limits are built into the relationship between belief and desire.

Hume distinguishes between two kinds of mental state: beliefs and desires. Beliefs attempt to represent what the world is actually like and can therefore be evaluated as true or false. Desires concern how the subject wants the world to be or to change. They cannot be evaluated in terms of truth or falsity.

Belief and desire can come apart. A person may want something without believing that it is good, appropriate, or likely to happen. Conversely, a person may believe something without wanting it.

Beliefs and desires therefore belong to different categories of mental state. The fact that someone has a particular desire is, first of all, a fact about their psychological condition.

In the Humean picture used here, reason can discover facts and calculate means. It can determine, for example, whether a particular spider is poisonous, and whether wearing gloves or keeping one’s distance would be the more effective way to avoid being bitten. Reason can correct beliefs and improve the means by which a goal is pursued, but it cannot create or eliminate desire from nothing. On this view, reason serves the passions rather than legislating for them.

Feminist critique can therefore change beliefs without necessarily changing desires. As psychological and bodily facts, desires are often already embodied by the time theory arrives. They cannot simply be declared mistaken, although we can still investigate the conditions under which they are produced and the consequences of acting upon them.

This also explains why someone may repost an article criticizing the ideal of pale skin, childlike youthfulness, and thinness, then feel genuine pleasure in front of the mirror when contouring makes their nose appear more defined. Their beliefs have changed, but their desires have not immediately withdrawn.

Yet neither Andrea Long Chu nor Hume fully answers the question of why norm-oriented desire exists in the first place. Why do our desires so often turn toward norms? Is this merely a psychological phenomenon, or is it rooted in deeper social structures?

## Durkheim: When Desire Has No Form, Anomie Emerges

The sociologist Émile Durkheim used the anomie of unmarried men to illustrate the social form of desire. Marriage regulates the sexual desire of married men by setting boundaries around it, assigning it a legitimate object, and giving it a predictable future. When a person understands what lies within their reach and recognizes that certain pursuits have lost their meaning, desire acquires a relatively definite scale.

An unmarried man faces a different situation. He is, in principle, free to desire anyone. This appears to grant him unlimited freedom, but it may also expand into a desire for everything. Desire lacks boundaries, and its object can always be replaced by some other possibility. Beyond every satisfaction remains an option that has not yet been tried. Desire consequently drifts without end, making it difficult to know what one truly wants or where desire should stop.

In the social context in which Durkheim lived, marriage held a near-monopoly over the legitimate institutional form of intimate relationships. He therefore observed that when desire loses the regulation of shared rules, it may fall into anomie: the person continues to want, but can no longer situate that desire within a predictable structure of reality. Durkheim was concerned not only with how prohibitions repress desire, but also with how social forms give desire limits and direction.

Drawing on Eva Illouz’s analysis in *The End of Love: A Sociology of Negative Relations*, we can extend this question into contemporary intimate life. Modern subjects possess greater freedom. They may enter relationships, but they may also leave them or withdraw their commitments. Partners and relationships are therefore more easily experienced as replaceable possibilities from which one can always withdraw. When desire lacks a form capable of carrying it, it may remain indefinitely within the imagination of possibility, unable to solidify into an actual choice.

In other words, for desire to become action rather than remain a feeling, it must be organized through social forms.

The most common and accessible forms in everyday life are usually institutions, norms, and roles. When someone wants to wear makeup, this often occurs because the first desirable bodily image made available to them by the existing culture is a feminized body already organized by norms.

This gives us the first explanation of norm-oriented desire: while searching for form, desire is intercepted by norms. Looking back, we may imagine that desire naturally points toward rules, institutions, and roles. More precisely, desire needs form, and norms frequently monopolize the supply of forms.

Why do discourses of post-identity, fluidity, and the abolition of definitions sound liberating and progressive, yet repeatedly fail to explain individual experience or enable people simply to leave norms behind in everyday life?

The key is that identity can itself be a social form of desire. Post-identity ideas ultimately belong to the domains of reason and belief. Desires sedimented over the course of a life do not automatically dissolve merely because someone has absorbed a theory.

When a person continuously dissects themselves through the language of post-identity, desires that were previously attached to the form of an identity may lose their object, their path, and the structures through which they receive recognition. The norm has been criticized, but desire is left suspended. It no longer knows where to go. What remains is pain without an object.

## Illouz: Desire Is Embedded in Visual Capitalism

If Durkheim explains why desire needs form, Illouz further demonstrates how visual capitalism supplies bodily desire with desirable objects and pathways for approaching them.

This provides a second explanation of norm-oriented desire: visual capitalism has deeply penetrated everyday experience, while real alternatives remain relatively impoverished.

Illouz illustrates this dynamic with a self-defined “sugar baby” blogger who describes the work she invests in her appearance in order to attract a rich man:

> Could I really be happy with a man old enough to be my father? Yes, yes and yes! You see, by being meticulously preened and fabulous at all times I give up nothing. I love coordinating my outfits, getting manicures, pedicures and especially shopping. I love all these things with or without the benefit of meeting rich men (preferably with). Looking my best gives me a high because I am a perfectionist to the core and nothing less will do. Besides, if I was married to a rich man I would be carrying on the same. […] The woman you see walking down the street is a piece of art. A lot of maintenance—manicures, pedicures, hair cuts, waxing, bleaching, plucking and SHOPPING—goes into that piece of art. Art reflects your lifestyle and status. If a trophy is what you want, then you must be prepared to pay.

Illouz comments:

> Beauty and sexual attractiveness are here predicated on the male gaze, and in turn require a preliminary intensive work of self fashioning grounded in the consumer sphere, to be recycled and circulated as an image of themselves, consumed by men who detain economic power. This sugar baby suggests that she is at once a sexual and economic agent, a consumer and producer of her own value.

Women often invest labor in continuous evaluation and comparison, remaining vulnerable to devaluation even as they derive pleasure, excitement, and a sense of agency from the process. The pleasure of makeup contains precisely this paradox: the process through which the body is captured by norms is also the process through which it enters a visible system of value. When someone looks in the mirror and finds herself more legibly feminine, more polished, and more desirable, what she may be experiencing is precisely the production of value from within visual capitalism.

Our desire to wear makeup and dress ourselves up undoubtedly exists. It is embedded in a broader economic system and cultural environment, but it is still genuinely experienced by the subject. Society shapes desire, and desire can nevertheless remain real. Its reality does not require an appeal to some pure essence of the soul.

The desire to make money provides a clear example. We understand that this desire partly comes from social norms. Yet money itself is embedded in systems of resource distribution, continually shaping where a person can live, what kind of education they can receive, and how much freedom of action they possess. Revealing the social origins of the desire for money does not enable someone to opt out of a social order structured by money. We can even openly discuss fantasies of getting rich overnight without first diagnosing ourselves as victims of false consciousness.

The desire to wear makeup and dress up works in a similar way. Visual capitalism connects appearance to material benefits and social evaluation. It also makes certain bodies more likely to occupy desirable positions within intimate relationships. At the same time, it can genuinely generate pleasure and anticipation.

Visual capitalism makes the path toward obtaining this value exceptionally concrete. The system has already prepared an entire world of objects for desire. A person can learn from an endless supply of images how a face might become more refined. Makeup techniques then transform that image into a set of executable steps, while mirrors and photographs repeatedly provide visible evidence of change. Desire thus acquires a clear object and a path through which that object can be approached.

Once we recognize that we are always already situated within this system and can find few alternative forms through which desire might be organized, desire no longer appears inexplicably drawn toward the norms of visual capitalism. It obtains value and pleasure within existing institutions and therefore struggles to imagine where else it might go.

The problem is that the production-consumption mechanisms of visual capitalism are extraordinarily effective at incorporating emerging alternatives. Some aesthetic experiments first created by people who rejected the mainstream eventually become a “niche style” within the fashion industry, once again transformed into consumer options that can be purchased and displayed. Even discourses of the “independent woman” and the Chinese figure of the **“自然女”**—a woman whose femininity appears natural, effortless, and unadorned—can easily be compressed into recognizable visual images on online platforms and subsequently absorbed into cycles of algorithmic distribution and symbolic packaging.

Perhaps people can still search for aesthetic practices that are smaller, more contingent, and more difficult for the market to reproduce reliably. Perhaps the key also lies in finding ways to step outside the position of being constantly evaluated. Yet creating new forms is difficult, and escaping existing positions is equally difficult. The resistance encountered in reality is far more stubborn than the fantasy that once we have understood something theoretically, we will be able to resist it effectively.

## Conclusion: Living With Norm-Oriented Desire

At this point, perhaps we can temporarily set aside the question of whether wearing makeup is progressive. The desire does not disappear simply because we have subjected it to critique. It is already there: embodied, but socially formed. To call it freedom without qualification would be careless. To dismiss it casually as false would be equally crude.

When I say that I want to wear makeup, I mean that I am drawn to a particular image of the body. This desire can be understood, and it can also be acted upon. Once it enters action, however, it raises more than one kind of question. Pleasure, feeling more at ease in one’s body, and greater confidence in particular settings all deserve consideration. At the same time, we should remain attentive to how these practices pull other bodies into the same system of evaluation, potentially producing new forms of shame and exclusion.

We might therefore return desire to the concrete situations of everyday life, observing how it emerges, how it affects action, and how it relates to other bodies.

For example, when I open a bottle of foundation before attending a gathering, I can ask myself three questions. After applying it, do I feel more eager to go out, or more anxious about being seen once the makeup begins to wear off? What image of womanhood and what standards of judgment sustain this anxiety, and how did I learn them? If I wore no makeup at all today, would the people accompanying me treat me differently?

These questions have no standard answers. Yet they allow desire to become a reason for action that can be described and examined, rather than an unexplained fact.

I can openly acknowledge that I want to become more beautiful and more visible, and that I want to encounter in the mirror a version of myself that is more readily legible as feminine. At the same time, I understand that this desire is not politically innocent. It comes from the imagery of visual capitalism, but it is also entangled with my own gendered life experience. At times it gives me pleasure. At other times it makes me more anxious and more critical of myself, and more inclined to surrender myself to the judgment of others.

Living with norm-oriented desire may therefore begin by distinguishing the object of our critique. We can criticize norms without treating desire itself as evidence of guilt. We can criticize the institutions and structures that produce and reward these norms without turning our hostility against the people who possess such desires, whether ourselves or those around us.

We should be especially wary when women and other people disadvantaged by the gender system attack and divide one another over whether someone is “progressive” enough. Once the critique of norms becomes an internal moral tribunal, its hostility can easily be redirected toward people who are also living within the same system. The result is endless internal depletion, while those who possess greater resources, continually produce norms, and profit from them recede from view. One measure of feminism is whether it refuses both to bar women from doing something because of their gender and to condemn them for choosing to do it.

If feminism intends to take embodied experience seriously, it cannot merely demand that people hold the “correct” beliefs. It must also confront the desires that have already sedimented in the body: desires that are shy, ordinary, pleasurable, and resistant to being flattened by theory.
