# The Body Theory Toolbox: An Introduction

## Remapping the Felt Topography of Trans Embodiment

This essay introduces the **Body Theory Toolbox**, a series that will continue to explore the questions and concepts outlined here.

Trans bodily experience has long been flattened into a familiar story: a true, deep, authentic soul inhabits the “wrong” body, and the incongruence between the two produces psychological distress. Since 2019, the World Health Organization has used the term **gender incongruence** for trans-related experiences in the ICD-11, moving the diagnosis out of the chapter on mental disorders and into the section on conditions related to sexual health. Formally, this change softens the charge of abnormality. Yet it preserves an underlying premise: that some objectively definable incongruence exists between the subject and their body, and that this incongruence must still be classified, named, and managed. It may be understood as a representative case of the “wrong body” narrative being absorbed into medical discourse.

Some deeply flawed conceptual systems within gender studies bear part of the responsibility, with the sex/gender distinction and the mind/body divide serving as cautionary examples. Within feminist theory, the concept of gender gained prominence as a way of challenging androcentric claims that women’s subordinate social position followed directly from their bodily constitution. In this sense, gender was, from the beginning, at least partly a defensive theoretical concept: sex appeared to name innate, stable, and immutable biological facts, while gender relocated certain gendered experiences into the acquired, mutable, and culturally shaped domain.

When gender scholars speak of these two concepts, a faint boundary often remains in the background of their thinking: sex on this side, gender on the other—even though different scholars may understand the two terms in radically different ways.

The sex/gender distinction contains a residue of dualism. Dualism is deeply rooted in the Western philosophical tradition. From Plato and Descartes to modern systems of rigid gender opposition, its influence can be found everywhere, stamped into thought itself. More specifically, sex and gender carry within them the divisions between culture and nature, mind and body, will and body. The former term in each pair is generally imagined as the more active and valuable side. The latter is expected to remain subordinate and serve the former, while frequently being marked as uncontrollable.

The history of trans medicine has remained remarkably “faithful” to this tidy mind/body logic. Tara Gonsalves’s study of the production of transgender medical knowledge in the United States shows that medicine has continually rewritten its understanding of “authentic gender.” Earlier medical judgments relied more heavily on external genitalia and family structure. They later shifted toward genes, brain structures, and individual inner experience, moving the location of gender ever further inward.

Surface sex characteristics were no longer treated as the sole source of truth. In their place emerged a more psychologized mode of judgment, one that pointed toward something supposedly deeper. This shift helped legitimize trans people’s first-person accounts of gender. Theoretically, however, it preserved the mind/body divide in a new form. The point of departure moved from the body itself to an inner standard through which the body would be interpreted and adjusted: on one side stood a gender continually internalized and understood as more profound; on the other stood a bodily surface to be calibrated and reshaped.

This residue of dualism is harmful. The vulgarization of sex in popular discourse owes much to the persistence of the mind/body divide. Feminist theorists have taken many different positions. Toril Moi argues that sex exists but should not determine one’s ways of living. Judith Butler places the interpretation of sex itself under sociocultural mediation. Iris Marion Young suggests that an individual’s experience of sex may combine what are conventionally understood as male and female experiences, while judgments about an individual’s sex contribute little to structural analysis.

None of these positions endorses the atavistic simplification that “you are biologically female or male, and therefore you must be such-and-such.” Yet this ghost is everywhere: in educational posts written by supposed allies, in the exclusionary rhetoric of TERFs, and in the normative language of medical systems.

For trans people, the mind/body divide drastically simplifies both painful and joyful gendered experience. It can feed a self-hating impulse in which one feels permanently illegitimate at some level for having failed to “transform” the body. Experiences of social rejection and stigma then become entangled with this painful struggle against the body, potentially intensifying the felt dislocation between body and mind.

Some interpretations of gender performativity within queer scholarship, as well as the now-mainstream paradigm of “doing gender,” can also reduce gendered comportment and expression to secondary products of cultural norms. Within such a framework, trans distress is diminished into an anxiety about failing to conform to gender conventions, while a whole range of embodied experiences is silenced.

At the same time, if gender is understood purely as a disembodied cultural construction, theory loses its basis for explaining the intimate connection between gender norms and idealized bodily forms—what everyday language calls “body shape.” In this sense, some queer theories’ inattention to trans experience, as well as their instrumental use of trans people as convenient illustrations of “resistance to the binary,” have already been challenged and criticized within trans studies.

What I hope to introduce and develop through this series is a theory of trans embodiment capable of moving beyond the sex/gender distinction. I consider such a framework indispensable for understanding trans bodily experience.

A supposedly “pure” psychological gender—or a psychologized gender identity—is often not independent of how one perceives one’s body. It frequently interacts with body image and body schema. Conversely, bodily perception is also shaped by psychological processes.

More fundamentally, our perception of our own bodies is never simply the body in a literal sense. The anatomical body does not pass directly into bodily experience. Lived bodily experience is itself an ambiguous register situated between sex and gender, biology and psychology, connecting the two sides of the division. It therefore gives us a powerful way to strike at the harms of mind/body dualism.

What kind of body theory, then, do we need? I propose nine exploratory criteria.

1. It should move beyond the mind/body divide and dissolve the dualistic residue of the sex/gender distinction.

2. It should avoid the purely social-constructionist tendencies found in some queer theories, keeping bodily factors under consideration and allowing the body to remain present.

3. It should attend not only to gender dysphoria or distress, but also to the importance of other affects within trans experience.

4. It should be capable of explaining the experiences of trans women and trans men while also doing justice to nonbinary and intersex experiences.

5. It should explain how alienation from, or aversion toward, particular organs may arise without falling into essentialism or treating any specific organ as the center of gender.

6. It should accommodate “this sex which is not one”: diffuse, non-unitary forms of sexuality. It should uncouple sexuality from sex and leave room for the entanglement of wanting and wanting to become, rather than treating trans experience as wholly unrelated to desire.

7. It should explain how bodily perception changes over the course of transition. Inertia and perceptual lag may produce forms of dislocation that gradually diminish with time.

8. It should move beyond the popular slogan that “clothes have no gender” and explain how gendered clothing participates in the production of bodily experience.

9. It should preserve the force of trans people’s first-person declarations of gender, without treating them either as expressions of a mysterious essence or as forms of “false consciousness.”

The theoretical materials I draw on come from broad traditions of psychoanalysis and the phenomenology of the body. My approach, however, is explicitly critical and selective. I intend to move past their worst phallocentric claims and gather the insights they offer into bodily experience.

From my reading so far, the central constellation of concepts includes **body image**, **body schema** or **postural schema**, **imaginary anatomy**, **sexual schema**, **libidinal cathexis**, and the **skin ego**.

These concepts pass first through the feminist reading offered by Elizabeth Grosz’s *Volatile Bodies*, a foundational work of corporeal feminism. I then reorganize them through a trans and queer feminist framework, while also drawing on Gayle Salamon’s arguments in *Assuming a Body*. The aim is to turn concepts developed through male-centered accounts of embodiment, nonfeminist theoretical traditions, and scholarship outside gender studies into a toolbox for a theory of trans embodiment—after removing their sexism, binary gender assumptions, cisheteronormativity, and the pervasive influence of homophobia and transphobia.

Even in 2026, rigorous works of body theory written by trans people for trans readers remain remarkably difficult to find.

After introducing a series of brilliant concepts with real explanatory power for trans experience, *Volatile Bodies* abruptly turns in its final chapter toward a crude, binary account of “sexual difference” and reveals a transphobic position—as though its intellectual standards collapse the moment trans experience enters the picture.

*Assuming a Body* has often been criticized for giving priority to theoretical coherence while incorporating too little of the density and diversity of trans experience.

This lamentable absence reveals the world’s ignorance, mystification, and indifference toward trans bodies. It also shows how complex and variable the question itself is, and how difficult it can be to grasp through language and concepts. It is far easier to speak of “gender identity” or “psychological gender.” But must trans bodily experience remain a black box forever?

Julia Serano’s work comes close to what is needed. Drawing on the dual intuitions of a biologist and a trans woman, she carved out a conceptual space between sex and gender through the idea of **subconscious sex**. The concept helps explain why, even under long-standing social norms, trans women may experience an irreconcilable discomfort with “living as men.”

For Serano, gender identity is not merely an identity at the level of gender. It is also connected to the perception of bodily sex characteristics and contains both conscious and subconscious dimensions. It therefore cannot be fully captured or disciplined through reason.

Yet Serano does not develop this concept through psychoanalytic or phenomenological vocabularies. As a result, subconscious sex remains theoretically ambiguous: it has genuine explanatory power, but its conceptual foundations remain underdeveloped, which has limited its wider uptake. Many areas of trans bodily experience therefore remain urgently in need of interpretation.

The **Body Theory Toolbox** aims to help more trans and queer people work through these difficult entanglements and respond more adequately to widespread trans experiences that have rarely been named.

For me, the central commitment must remain problem-oriented. Trans experience should take priority over theory’s drive to elaborate itself. At the same time, this work should keep its distance from academic writing that turns jargon and cultural capital into ends in themselves.

Throughout the series, I therefore hope to make clarity and accessibility the first priority without sacrificing scholarly rigor. After all, **“we write for us” should never be merely a slogan.**
