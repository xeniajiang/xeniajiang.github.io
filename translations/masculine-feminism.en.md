# Why We Should Be Wary of “Masculine Feminism”

## On Bottom Shame and Transmisogyny

It pains me to see some people online who claim to “speak up for women” uncritically use stigmatizing terms such as “br0” against cisgender heterosexual men. Yet those most likely to become collateral damage are people who have complicated relationships with femininity but do not occupy positions of patriarchal advantage. More troublingly, many feminist theorists, intentionally or otherwise, repeat the following pattern in their writing: they imagine a set of personality traits, dispositions, or bodily characteristics representing “traditional femininity” or “femininity”; they regard these traits as products of discipline under male domination; and they therefore argue that women should do their utmost to cast them off on the path toward structural equality. If a woman takes pleasure in pursuing femininity, that pleasure is likely to be dismissed as patriarchal “false consciousness.” **And yet, how could feminism and femininity possibly be irreconcilable?**

## 1. Masculine Feminism and “False Consciousness”

Here, I provisionally use “masculine feminism” as an analytical term for the tendency, in theory or practice, to emphasize breaking away from “traditional femininity” or “femininity.” Feminism has long worked to dismantle any necessary connection between biological traits and gendered dispositions. This is an important theoretical advance. But when “women’s liberation” becomes bound to “moving away from femininity,” and masculinity is treated as a prerequisite for power, prestige, and freedom, feminism can unwittingly reaffirm the patriarchal value hierarchy in which masculinity ranks above femininity. In online culture, the mechanism of bottom shame exemplified by terms such as “br0” shows precisely how the devaluation of femininity continues to operate through language and affect.

In much contemporary feminist discourse, **“false consciousness” is often used as an overly convenient diagnostic tool.** Whenever someone expresses a love of femininity, beauty, or adornment, that love can easily be explained away as the outcome of patriarchal discipline, as though the subject’s own feelings need not be taken seriously. Yet this mode of judgment inadvertently perpetuates a familiar patriarchal logic: **it assumes that individuals cannot reliably understand their own desires and pleasures and must instead depend on an external, more “clear-sighted” authority.** Such a posture can alienate people in very different social positions, each with complex bodily experiences of their own. It may also turn feminism from a language of liberation addressed to all marginalized groups into an insular discourse with a higher threshold for entry and a narrower set of demands. A genuinely liberatory vision may require us to understand difference more patiently, so that every bodily feeling and choice can be taken seriously.

## 2. Femininity as Scapegoat

In *Whipping Girl: A Transsexual Woman on Sexism and the Scapegoating of Femininity* (2007), Julia Serano explicitly introduces the concept of “transmisogyny” as a concentrated manifestation of misogyny. She distinguishes between “oppositional sexism” and “traditional sexism.” Oppositional sexism holds that men and women are profoundly different and excludes any form of existence that crosses this divide. **Traditional sexism, by contrast, is grounded in the patriarchal ranking of gendered dispositions and continually reproduces the prejudice that masculinity is superior to femininity.** This hierarchy is embedded in our social values, leaving countless people who occupy feminized positions or embody femininity systematically devalued and disregarded. Trans women face intersecting forms of discrimination because they shatter the fantasy of a strict and inviolable gender binary while also “proudly” or “willingly” expressing their femininity.

**Serano explicitly issues a theoretical call to “bring femininity back into feminism”:**

> Granted, this idea—that feminism and femininity are in opposition to one another—has often been fostered by those who wish to undermine feminism. For several decades now, feminism’s opponents have attempted to dissuade women from the movement by repeating two (seemingly contradictory) sound bites: that feminists are “man-haters” (read: “homosexual”) while simultaneously “wanting to be men” (read: “masculine”). While one cannot underestimate the negative effect that this antifeminist propaganda has had in turning feminine and heterosexual women away from feminism, we would be doing ourselves a great disservice if we didn’t also acknowledge the fact that many feminists themselves have forwarded the idea that femininity is artificial and incompatible with feminism. This antifemininity tendency may represent the feminist movement’s single greatest tactical error. It’s high time we rectify this mistake by purposefully putting the feminine back into feminism.

## 3. Are There Intrinsic Inclinations in Gendered Dispositions?

As both a biologist and a transgender woman, Serano writes from an anti-essentialist position while also exploring the body’s **“intrinsic inclinations.”** She treats gendered dispositions as one part of these bodily inclinations. Like every reasonable feminist, however, she does not argue that biological traits determine temperament, behavior, or positions of power. Rather, social construction must take shape in and through bodies with particular characteristics. To be honest, this is not an easy question to handle: take the idea one step too far, and “intrinsic inclination” seems liable to slip into some kind of “biological essence.” Yet an intrinsic inclination need not be understood as a biological essence. It can instead be understood as a stable pattern within embodied experience.

Here is an analogy that may make this easier to understand. In popular culture, the MBTI type INFP is often associated with a certain feminine disposition: INFPs are introverted, guided by intuition rather than concrete sensation, emotionally sensitive, and more inclined toward spontaneity than planning. The highly sensitive person (HSP) trait is also sometimes treated as feminine. **If we were speaking of a personality category rather than a “disposition” forcibly tied to gender, we would be less likely to invoke a patriarchally coded hierarchy of value and less likely to criticize an individual’s disposition**—after all, the sixteen personality types appear equally worthy of respect. We would not say, “You are so INFP and identify so strongly with it that you must be suffering from false consciousness produced by patriarchal discipline.” Nor would we take it for granted that “an ESTJ personality is more conducive to independence and self-liberation than an INFP personality is.”

As with personality, then, what structural forces name as gendered dispositions need not possess any deep essence. They may be contingent, yet still take on real form in particular individuals. Masculine and feminine dispositions are distributed across the population. Some bodily and psychological states must make particular masculine or feminine dispositions more likely to emerge. This has nothing to do with biological determinism. It simply takes into account the realities of embodied life alongside the effects of social construction. Just as INFP traits are not regarded as “false consciousness,” femininity, as a possible embodied “intrinsic inclination,” need not itself be pathologized or demeaned. **Should people whose bodies are intrinsically inclined toward femininity, and whose unconscious desires turn toward feminine ways of being, therefore be treated as inferior? If we cannot reflect on this prejudice in theory, how can we ensure that we do not become accomplices to patriarchal “traditional sexism”?**

## 4. Beauty as Discipline: Exposure and Misreading

Some time ago, while writing a paper on footwear, clothing, and gender discipline, I read a series of articles on fashion, dress, beauty, cosmetic surgery, and weight loss. I joked at the time that I might be about to confront some of the people most critical of “the structures that associate women with beauty.” In *Femininity*, Susan Brownmiller devotes an entire book to analyzing in meticulous detail how femininity is produced through discipline. I greatly admire the concreteness and precision of her writing. Yet she repeatedly lapses back into essentialist ideas, causing parts of her argument to unravel, while prejudices against queer and trans people also surface between the lines. Queer readers should proceed with caution. *Unbearable Weight*, *The Beauty Myth*, and Maura Gancitano’s *Specchio delle mie brame: La prigione della bellezza* (*Mirror, Mirror: The Prison of Beauty*, published in Chinese as 《服美役》) all powerfully show how implicit standards of beauty and thinness shape women’s everyday experiences of disempowerment. Thoughtful theorists have tried to rescue women from the painful world of “all women should love beauty” and “women must be fair-skinned, youthful, and thin”—and this work has gradually produced subtle but real change. Yet when arguments about “beauty as a social norm” are interpreted without sufficient care, they can slide in another direction: toward the claim that beauty-loving, feminine people—not only women, but also people who do not identify as women—are never “natural,” but merely patriarchal artifacts. **This reduces complex subjective experience to a pure product of power and thereby denies individuals both agency and the genuine pleasure they may find in such practices.**

In *The Second Sex*, Simone de Beauvoir uses an existentialist perspective to call on people situated as women to pursue “transcendence” and remain wary of the “immanence” produced by disempowerment, thereby realizing the infinite possibilities of female existence. But in the course of interpretation—or as this argument hardens into a theoretical intuition—some readers bind femininity tightly to “immanence” and conclude that only masculine people possess subjectivity and transcendence. This is plainly a misreading.

## 5. Feminine Bodies and the Subjective Pleasure of “Recovering Our Clothes”

Anyone seeking a deeper understanding of embodied femininity should read Iris Marion Young’s *Throwing Like a Girl*. The Simplified Chinese edition is currently out of stock; a Traditional Chinese edition is available, and the English is not especially difficult with translation support. **Young offers a profound phenomenological description of “feminine bodily comportment”; her theory also helps us understand how women learn to keep their legs together, how they must inhabit a world in which men spread their legs and occupy space, what forms of bodily perception emerge from living in a feminine body, and to what extent that body is a subject or an object/Other.**

The essay that most surprised me in the collection was “Women Recovering Our Clothes.” Young is not satisfied with explaining women’s clothing solely through the “male gaze.” Instead, she asks: **How might we redescribe women’s love of clothes?**

> Patriarchal fashion folds create a meticulous paradigm of the woman well dressed for the male gaze, then endows with guilt the pleasure we might derive for ourselves in these clothes. Misogynist mythology gloats in its portrayal of women as frivolous body decorators. Well-trained to meet the gaze that evaluates us for our finery, for how well we show him off, we then are condemned as sentimental, superficial, duplicitous, **because we attend to and sometimes learn to love the glamorous arts.**

Beyond exposing the misogyny embedded in this discipline, Young identifies three forms of pleasure that women can derive from clothing: **touch, bonding, and fantasy.** Drawing on Luce Irigaray, she writes that feminine desire “moves through the medium of touch more than sight.” Less concerned with identifying things, comparing them, or measuring them against one another, touch immerses the subject in fluid continuity with the object. For the touching subject, the object touched seems to touch back, blurring the border between self and other.

> **Some of our clothes we love for their own sake, because their fabric and cut and color charm us and relate to our bodies in specific ways—because, I almost want to say, they love us back.** Those wool-blend pinstriped elephant-bottom pants that held a crease so well and flopped so happily around my ankles. The green herringbone wool blazer I made with my own hands and, after the lining fell apart, I sadly gave it to my sister because the new lining was too small. The wine-red-print full-sleeved smooth rayon blouse, gathered at the shoulders to drape lightly over my chest.…

Young’s passages on the “fantasy” of clothing are equally arresting:

> **There is a certain freedom involved in our relation to clothes**, an active subjectivity not represented in the male-gaze theory. Here I draw on Sartre but not his gaze theory. In *The Psychology of Imagination*, Sartre proposes imaginary consciousness as a modality of freedom. An image is consciousness of an unreal object. In imagining, I am aware of an unreal object and aware that the object is unreal. The pleasure of imagining derives from just this unreality, for the unreal object has no facticity, no givenness that constrains us, no brute physicality that freedom must deal with or face the consequences.
>
> There are ways of looking at oneself in the mirror that do not appraise oneself before the objectifying gaze, but rather desubstantialize oneself, turn oneself into a picture, an image, an unreal identity. In such fantasy we do not seek to be somebody else. Fantasizing is not wishing, hoping, or planning; it has no future. The clothing image provides the image of situations without any situatedness; there is an infinite before and after; **thus the images are open at both ends to an indefinite multitude of possible transformations.**
>
> **One of the privileges of femininity in rationalized instrumental culture is an aesthetic freedom**, the freedom to play with shape and color on the body, to don various styles and looks, and through them exhibit and imagine unreal possibilities. Women often actively indulge in such theatrical imagining, which is largely closed to the everyday lives of men or which they live vicariously through the clothes of women. Such female imagination has liberating possibilities because it subverts, unsettles the order of respectable, functional rationality in a world where that rationality supports domination. The unreal that wells up through imagination always creates the space for a negation of what is, and thus the possibility of alternatives.

For a feminine life that loves beauty, recognizes itself in high sensitivity and “INFP,” lingers over clothes and makeup, and yet repeatedly puts itself on trial—wondering whether it is merely a form of “false consciousness” produced by patriarchal discipline or an artificial construct—this is surely a gentle but powerful antidote. **Ultimately, if feminism is truly to escape patriarchal hierarchy, perhaps it should not lead women into a masculine order. It should instead question the order that puts different prices on masculinity and femininity in the first place.**
