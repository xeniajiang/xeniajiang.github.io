# How Many Layers Does “Gender” Have?

## A Conceptual Inventory for Understanding Our Own Gender

**At a glance:** When we talk about “gender,” what exactly are we talking about? This article takes apart a concept that has long been treated as a single package. It distinguishes three layers within “gender”: social interaction, embodied experience, and assignment and recognition, and explains how these layers come to be connected by default.

This article introduces many concepts. It can be read closely when you have an uninterrupted stretch of time, or used as a reference toolbox. A short set of questions at the end can be used for self-reflection or as a starting point for further thought.

## 1. “Gender” as an All-in-One Package

Whenever we speak of “gender,” whether we mean *sex* or *gender*, it is easy to sense a certain linguistic ambiguity: the same word is used to refer to entirely different things. Sometimes it refers to bodily traits, sometimes to social roles, sometimes to self-identification, and sometimes simply to how others address or describe you. Most people do not notice this. The conflation is so ordinary that it has nearly become a default setting.

In my view, this polysemy is itself one of the ways the binary gender system sustains itself. Different kinds of content are bundled into a single word, like an all-in-one software package: everything is accepted with one click at installation, each component calls on the others during operation, and over time we forget that they could ever have been separated.

Once we grow accustomed to using “gender” to encompass everything, we gradually lose the ability to distinguish among its layers. Different dimensions are assumed to correspond to one another, forming an entire set of implicit “assumptions of connection.” These assumptions need not be stated, yet they continue to operate, making a complex structure appear to be a natural whole.

This article takes apart the bundled concept of “gender.” I proceed through several principal layers—Social Interaction, Embodied Experience, and Assignment & Recognition—identifying the terms and mechanisms associated with each. The aim is to peel back and slice through this mille-feuille of a concept, layer by layer. Rather than rushing to answer “What is a person’s gender?”, we first ask: what are the distinct things that we ordinarily call “gender” all at once?

In an earlier article, I discussed why the distinction between *sex* and *gender* is not absolute and how it carries the problems of mind–body dualism. A phenomenology of the body is therefore needed to examine how the embodied, experiential, living body—the *lived body*—becomes gendered. In addition to retaining Social Interaction associated with *gender* and Assignment & Recognition associated with *sex*, I introduce Embodied Experience. This gives bodily feelings and first-person experiences, previously compressed into conceptual gaps, a place where they can be separately recognized and described.

## 2. Social Interaction

This is the layer that gender theorists often call *gender*. Yet the concept of *gender* itself bundles together too much. Anything classified as social, cultural, relational, acquired, or constructed tends to be placed within it. Precisely because the concept contains so much, it can impede clear self-understanding and self-expression. Meanwhile, the misleading term “gender identity” compounds the confusion.

In my framework, following a line of thought inspired by Julia Serano, gender identity is not understood solely as something social or cultural. It is closer to a form of assignment and recognition concerning the self, while also being entangled with embodied experience, social interaction, institutional classification, and other layers.

**Gendered Appearance & Readability:** Often discussed in terms of gender expression or gendered disposition, this dimension concerns both *expression* and *qizhi*: the former emphasizes active presentation, while the latter refers to the overall gendered impression conveyed by a person’s appearance, bearing, and style. These terms capture two sides of social interaction: performance and reading.

**Identity & Role:** The relevant term here is *role*: gendered divisions of labor, roles, and responsibilities that are often relational. This concerns the social position into which a person is placed and the duties, labor, and emotional functions they are expected to perform. Positions such as “daughter,” “husband,” “mother,” and “breadwinner” each carry an entire script.

**Spatial Orientation:** The key terms are *orientation* / *spatiality*. This concerns how a person locates themselves in gendered spaces; how they orient themselves toward particular objects, places, and paths through life; and how they are permitted, obstructed, made comfortable, or made uneasy in bathrooms, changing rooms, dormitories, streets, intimate relationships, and public settings. This draws on Sara Ahmed’s account of queer orientation.

**Interactive Behavior:** The key terms are *interaction* / *behavior*. This includes gender-coded ways of acting in everyday interaction: tone of voice, bodily movement, emotional expression, ways of caring for others, and ways of avoiding conflict or enacting oppression. The main point of reference here is the symbolic interactionist tradition in sociology, which produced the well-known theory of “doing gender” and is also especially prominent in Erving Goffman’s work, including “The Arrangement Between the Sexes.”

**Gendered Situation:** The relevant term is *situation*. This refers to the concrete ways a person finds themselves within situations structured by gendered power relations: being gazed at, belittled, sexualized, presumed to be a caregiver, or treated as a potential threat. The concept of “situation” comes primarily from existentialist feminism and appears in the work of Simone de Beauvoir and Toril Moi.

**Sexual Script:** This refers to default role arrangements and behavioral expectations within intimate relationships, desire, and sexual practice: who should initiate, who should be pursued, who is expected to submit, and who is permitted to express desire. For a long time, one sexual script has been overwhelmingly dominant in society. It functions almost like a myth about sexuality, as if every other arrangement of desire were merely an exception or a failure. Butler’s “heterosexual matrix” reveals how this myth operates: under the script of compulsory heterosexuality, gender is always assumed to lead toward a standardized form of heterosexual desire.

**Gendering Through Language:** This concerns how language places a person in a gendered position through pronouns, names, forms of address, and other default ways of referring to them. These may appear minor, yet they exert an intensely ordinary and powerful force of recognition. For some, they provide a reassuring sense of belonging; for others, they produce a misalignment into which they cannot comfortably fit.

## 3. Embodied Experience

If we remain only at the level of Social Interaction, we miss a crucial issue: the body is not only viewed and named as a third-person object; it also enters experience as a first-person existence. A phenomenological account of “gendered” experience is therefore essential.

**Lived Body Experience:** A central concept in phenomenology of the body, this refers to the body as it enters experience in the mode of “I am living.” From a first-person perspective, the subject feels and uses the body, remaining continuously connected to it through action and perception. The body appears in experience as “my body.” Iris Young explicitly argues that descriptions of an individual’s gender should be located at this level. In phenomenological terms, a person’s experience can simultaneously contain masculine, feminine, and difficult-to-classify dimensions.

This classification of bodily experience is inseparable from the material body, but it also contains boundaries imposed by society and culture. If some experiences are named masculine or feminine, that naming itself bears traces of construction. The same applies to the processes through which other phenomenological concepts of the body become “gendered.”

**Sexed Traits:** These are bodily traits read and classified as signs of gender difference in everyday life, such as height or waist-to-hip ratio, including features designated as “sex characteristics.” They are frequently used to support gender judgments and, in many contexts, to imply a gender binary. The reading, classification, and shaping of these traits are themselves ways in which culture acts upon the body.

**Body Schema:** Also known as *corporeal schema*, this is the bodily familiarity formed through action that allows a person to adjust movement and posture without deliberate thought. Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s phenomenology of the body is one important source for the concept. In *Throwing Like a Girl*, Iris Young examines the formation of a feminine body schema: some women hold their bodies back and restrict their range of movement when throwing. Although this is often treated as a “natural” feature of female bodies, it is actually a way of using the body formed through prolonged experience. Body schema is shaped through such repeated actions and can change across different environments and normative contexts.

**Body Image:** The concept comes primarily from the psychoanalyst Paul Schilder and was later widely adopted in psychology to explain the formation of various forms of bodily anxiety. It refers to the way the body appears in experience as “my body.” It is an image of the body organized from tactile, visual, postural, and other perceptual materials, while also being connected to consciousness, imagination, and sociocultural intervention. Body image opens an analytical layer between mind and body, making bodily experience irreducible to a purely physiological or anatomical object. It therefore often contains tension: it presents the body as currently experienced while also accommodating the subject’s extension toward an ideal form.

**Imaginary Anatomy:** The concept can first be approached through the “phantom limb.” After losing a limb, some people continue to feel that the hand or leg is still present and may even experience pain, itching, or an awkward posture. Phantom limbs remind us that the body is not experienced according to supposedly “objective anatomy.” The subject also carries an internal structural sense of what “my body should be like.” This develops through identification, mirror experience, and the gaze of others, giving the body a sense of coherence, boundaries, and wholeness in experience. It also helps shape body image and can produce tension: when the bodily structure a person feels or identifies with diverges from their physical body or its social reading, that difference may be experienced continuously.

**Sexual Schema:** The concept emerges from phenomenological discussions in the tradition of Merleau-Ponty. It refers to how desire operates in the body and reorganizes bodily experience as the body turns toward others and the world. Many feminist philosophers have argued that sexual experience is a mode of bodily sensitivity—a way of feeling the proximity of others. Building on this, Sara Ahmed proposes that “sexual orientation” concerns not only the object of desire but also how a person inhabits the world in a particular posture when that desire is expressed.

Sexual schema operates at this level. It gives the body direction and tension, carrying a person beyond a state of enclosure within the self and allowing them to experience the body’s extension, usability, and habitability in relation to others. It often begins in particular bodily regions and then diffuses through bodily experience as a whole. What desire ultimately changes, then, is how a person lives their body. Through this movement toward others, the body becomes a vehicle for reaching the world. Compared with “body schema,” this layer places greater emphasis on how directionality, attraction, and proximity between subjects rewrite bodily experience within relationships.

**Psychological Response Patterns:** The relevant term is *psychological traits*. During the twentieth century, “psychological gender” was once a popular concept. Early research attempted to describe individual differences through “masculine / feminine” personality traits. The Masculinity-Femininity (Mf) scale of the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI), for example, was initially used in part to identify homosexual tendencies, reflecting the medical and social context of its time. By the 1970s, this approach had developed into “sex-role orientation,” producing multiple scales that attempted to locate individuals through sets of traits culturally marked as “masculine” or “feminine.”

This framework later receded. On the one hand, it depended on bundles of stereotyped traits that could not support a stable theoretical structure. On the other, the scales produced inconsistent measurements. Even so, the habit of connecting personality traits to gender remains widespread, continuing to operate in more ordinary forms.

## 4. Assignment & Recognition

This section understands “assignment” in a broad sense as a mechanism for placing people within gender. It includes not only gender classifications made by official institutions at birth or when a gender marker is changed, but also registration and classification in organizational settings such as schools and companies, other people’s recognition of and insistence on a particular gender within social relationships, and the subject’s naming and acknowledgment of their own identity. Assignment thus refers to an ongoing process through which a person is placed—or places themselves—within a gendered position.

In this sense, any interaction that establishes a person’s gender identity with near-coercive force and makes it difficult for them to depart from that identity can be understood as an act of “assignment” or “recognition.” For this reason, the “affirmation” and “recognition” central to identity politics do not naturally stand outside power. Once institutionalized, they too can solidify identity boundaries and reproduce stable, clearly defined, and manageable subjects in the name of support.

**Coercive State Assignment:** The relevant terms are *assigned sex/gender at birth* and *sex/gender reassignment*. This refers to the classification of an individual’s gender by state, medical, and legal systems at birth or other institutional junctures. Assignment operates as though a single designation could fix a person for life, compressing complex bodies into limited categories for administrative convenience. This is especially apparent for people on the intersex spectrum, whose bodily traits may not fit neatly into binary boxes even as institutions continue to demand a single answer.

**Institutional Registration:** This refers to the way gender fields are completed, stored, and retrieved in schools, companies, hospitals, platforms, and form-based systems. Such registration sometimes matches coercive state assignment and sometimes allows a degree of flexibility, such as different forms of address, notes about preferences, or temporary accommodations. Yet this flexibility often forces individuals to move continually between systems and bear the burden of explaining inconsistent identities.

**Relational Identity Fixation:** Once a person has been perceived as a particular gender within a relationship, that judgment continues to operate as a prior assumption. Understandings of a person’s gender are often formed at first sight or during an initial meeting. Subsequent updates then resemble a highly conservative Bayesian process: new information is absorbed into and reinterpreted through the old judgment, rarely dislodging the initial perception. If someone first appears as a girl, for example, cutting their hair short later is more likely to be understood as “a girl with short hair.”

**Self-Assignment / Gender Identity:** Here, *self-assignment* refers to the subject’s active self-placement and self-recognition within a gendered position. It overlaps with what is commonly described as *self-identification* or *gender identity*. It remains entangled with embodied experience, social interaction, and institutional classification, sometimes receiving support from these layers and sometimes coming into conflict with them.

## 5. Implicit Norms & Layer Dynamics

This system depends on an entire set of implicit premises and norms, including assumptions of connection, alignment, and fixity. Through permeation, overlap, and power dynamics in the interactions among layers, these premises form a system of “gender forces” that produces and sustains the stable operation of the binary gender order.

**Assumptions of Connection:** An entire set of “assumptions of connection” allows different layers to be treated as though they should align. These assumptions remain quietly embedded in the background of social life and are therefore difficult to notice. They continually bind appearance, role, desire, experience, and assignment together, making a multilayered structure appear to be a naturally unified whole.

When a person is assigned female, for example, that assignment is quickly read as a presumption about a particular kind of body—chromosomes, a high-pitched voice, curves—and extended into expectations about appearance and disposition—femininity. These forms of readability are then connected to common social roles and divisions of labor—mother, caregiver—repeatedly recognized and reproduced in interaction through acts of care, and continually fixed in language through forms of address and reference—“she,” “Ms.” Over time, the fit among these layers is projected onto desire, which is understood as naturally leading toward a normative relationship form—desire for men. This continuous path from assignment through body, appearance, role, interaction, and language to desire then makes the original assignment appear to be a natural starting point.

Every leap between two layers requires an “assumption of connection.” Some take the form of coercive gender norms, some of covert shaping packaged by culture as “self-realization,” and some of stubborn stigma and stereotypes. At the methodological level, analyzing these assumptions between layers provides a key to understanding how the binary gender system continues to operate.

**Assumption of Alignment:** The stability of this multilayered system depends first on the assumption of alignment, according to which the layers should line up with one another.

**Assumption of Fixity:** It also depends on the assumption of fixity, according to which an alignment, once formed, should remain stable.

Their opposites are (1) misalignment between layers and (2) movement within layers: different layers no longer change in synchrony, or a single layer is itself in motion.

The formation of queer identities and active resistance takes place on the other side of these two assumptions—in misalignment between layers and movement within them. One can break the “assumption of alignment” and become a fissure in the gender system, or break the “assumption of fixity” and become the subject of the sentence: “You are fluid; do not stop.”

**Interactions Among Layers:** The layers permeate and overlap. Some content appears across multiple layers, while other elements become entangled in ways that resist neat separation. Power also operates differently across layers. In a particular setting, one layer may override others and temporarily determine how a person is understood and treated.

**Gender Forces:** These dynamics can be further abstracted into a new concept proposed here: gender forces. These gender forces are recurring modes of operation that run across the layers: performance and reading, experience and adjustment, assignment and recognition, and the punishment and correction that accompany deviation. Through the repeated action of these forces, a multilayered structure that could otherwise loosen is continually pulled back into an order that appears natural but must in fact be maintained.

## 6. Applications

**Case 1: Performativity—from “being seen” to “being remembered by the body”**

In common interpretations of Judith Butler’s “performativity,” gender is often understood as a social effect sustained through repeated enactments in appearance, language, and behavior. This interpretation emphasizes Social Interaction, particularly the cycle of performance and reading, making gender appear to be a surface form that can be repeatedly reproduced.

From the perspective of Embodied Experience, however, repetition does not remain external. Repeated postures, movements, and modes of interaction gradually sink into the body and become part of Body Schema and Body Image. “Acting like a woman,” then, is not only an outcome of being read by others; it also becomes a way the body uses itself, occupies space, and experiences itself.

This case reveals the permeation between Social Interaction and Embodied Experience: norms enter the body through repetition, and the body in turn makes those norms appear natural. Gender’s supposed “naturalness” often emerges within this cycle.

**Case 2: A person on the intersex spectrum—a rupture in the assumption of alignment**

Imagine a person on the intersex spectrum whose bodily traits and Embodied Experience tend overall toward femininity. In everyday interaction, she is consistently read as a woman and can live comfortably as one. In most settings, she may even conceal her gender history and be regarded as an ordinary cisgender woman. Within Assignment & Recognition, however, she was registered as male at birth, and the relevant documents are difficult to change. This conflicts with her lived reality only at a small number of institutional junctures.

The layers are clearly misaligned. Embodied Experience and Social Interaction largely align, while Assignment & Recognition remains elsewhere and retains an overriding power of interpretation at critical moments. The misalignment does not continuously produce conflict in daily life, but institutional junctures abruptly magnify it, forcing the person to bear the consequences of an assignment that has long since diverged from her lived reality.

This case first shows that institutionally assigned gender does not necessarily reflect or predict a person’s bodily traits (*sexed traits*); institutional assignment is a classificatory decision. It also unsettles the apparent boundary between cis and trans. In experience and interaction, the person occupies an experiential and social position close to that of a cisgender woman, yet cannot fully enter that category, while her experiential trajectory also differs from a typical transgender narrative.

Institutional gender fields are nearly “invisible” in daily life but create temporary difficulty at institutional junctures. This illustrates the power dynamics among layers: interaction and embodiment sometimes predominate, while at other times a person becomes trapped by institutionally imposed force. Her self-identification must not be overlooked. It acts as a force of recognition, transforming the embodied potential to live and be recognized “as a female body” into a social reality, while also fixing her firmly within a female identity in the eyes of most people.

**Case 3: “Women” flattened into one category—three different positions under a shared name**

Consider three other positions: a Tomboy (or a masculine lesbian), a feminist, and a housewife. Their lived practices and structures of experience differ substantially. A Tomboy’s appearance and interactive behavior often point toward a distinctly masculine style. The position of the feminist is defined more by reflection on and criticism of gender norms. The housewife occupies a highly gendered division of labor closely tied to care work and domestic roles.

Within this model, the three positions are distributed across different layers. Some center on Gendered Appearance & Readability and Interactive Behavior within Social Interaction. Some center on reflection and criticism of gender norms. Others lie in Identity & Role and divisions of labor within Social Interaction. They do not necessarily share the same Embodied Experience, imply the same arrangement of desire, or correspond to the same path through life.

Yet in everyday language, all three are easily called “women.” Here the word does not carefully distinguish among layers. It bundles appearance, role, standpoint, and experience together, compressing differences distributed across multiple layers into a single label. Through this compression, “gender” operates as an all-in-one package: it provides an apparently stable mode of classification while concealing the internal differentiation of its structure.

**Conclusion:** By separating three central layers of the concept of gender—social interaction, embodied experience, and assignment and recognition—this article reveals the implicit norms and the operation of “gender forces” that sustain the binary gender system. It takes apart the ambiguity and bundling performed by the word “gender.” I hope this conceptual inventory offers a clear analytical tool for understanding our own genders and reflecting on gender norms, as well as an operational framework for layered thinking in gender research.

## Appendix: A Reflective Inventory for Understanding Your Own Gender

Drawing on the central concepts above, I worked with an AI assistant to create a set of questions that readers can use to take apart and reconstruct their understanding of their own “gender.” Recalling gender-related experiences may cause discomfort. If you feel uncomfortable while answering, skip the question or stop.

**(1) Social Interaction**

**Gendered Appearance & Readability:** When you imagine how others see you, which outward features matter most to you as cues others may use to judge your gender? How would you like those features to be understood and perceived?

**Identity & Role:** When relational roles such as caregiver, supporter, subordinate, or decision-maker fall to you, do you experience them as bound to gender? Does that connection feel comfortable, awkward, or irrelevant?

**Spatial Orientation:** When entering gender-segregated spaces such as bathrooms, changing rooms, or dormitories, does where you “should go” feel clear and certain, hesitant and uncertain, or distant and detached? Where does that feeling primarily come from?

**Interactive Behavior:** Do you actively adjust your tone of voice, gait, posture, facial expression, or bodily movement to approach or distance yourself from a gendered presentation? Does this adjustment feel more like voluntary choice, long-standing habit, or self-protection?

**Gendered Situation:** In everyday life, do you anticipate being placed in a gendered position—expected to care, explain, obey, protect, or restrain your speech and behavior? How do you feel about and respond to these expectations?

**Sexual Script:** When imagining intimate relationships and interactions of desire, do default divisions of roles or rhythms of interaction arise automatically? Do you fit, resist, or move ambivalently around the dominant sexual script?

**Gendering Through Language:** In your inner dialogue, which self-reference, pronouns, or forms of address feel most natural? Do they feel fitting and comfortable, reluctantly acceptable, or blank and indeterminate?

**(2) Embodied Experience**

**Lived Body Experience:** When you are alone and entirely outside social situations, does your body carry a gendered feeling of its own, or a bodily disposition that is difficult to classify? Is this internal sense of gender stable, variable, or nearly absent?

**Sexed Traits:** When sensing your body, do you spontaneously classify certain regions or traits as masculine, feminine, or indeterminate? Does this binary understanding make you feel relaxed and expansive, tense and uneasy, or conflicted?

**Body Schema:** In movements such as stretching, stepping, exerting force, or occupying space, does your body instinctively tend to open and extend or to hold back and restrain itself? Do you think this habitual use of the body is related to your gendered experience?

**Body Image:** When you form an inner picture of “my body,” which regions feel most clearly present? Which feel vague, unfamiliar, or even unwanted as part of that image? How does this internal body image relate to your sense of gender?

**Imaginary Anatomy:** In imagination, dreams, fantasy, or internal bodily sensation, has your sense of your bodily structure ever differed from your physical anatomy? Does this inner bodily imagination feel naturally reassuring, abruptly strange, comforting and healing, or psychologically burdensome?

**Sexual Schema:** When you experience attraction, desire, or an impulse toward intimacy, does your body register orientation, tension, a wish to approach, or an impulse to withdraw? Do these bodily changes alter your understanding of your own gendered position?

**Psychological Response Patterns:** When observing how you handle emotion, conflict, intimacy, competition, vulnerability, or care for others, do you connect these behavioral tendencies with labels such as masculinity or femininity? Does that connection feel accurate, restrictive, or irrelevant?

**(3) Assignment & Recognition**

**Coercive State Assignment:** Looking back at the official determination and registration of your gender at birth, how do you understand the long-term effects of that initial assignment? If your legal gender has changed, what has that change meant to you?

**Institutional Registration:** In schools, workplaces, hospitals, online platforms, and form-based systems, what gender are you officially recorded as, recognized as, and treated as? Does that institutional record fit, function as a compromise, or continually create friction?

**Relational Identity Fixation:** Do people around you continue to rely on their first impression of your gender without updating it? How do you live with these fixed and outdated perceptions?

**Self-Assignment / Gender Identity:** Considering how others have classified you in the past, your current institutional gender arrangements, and your expectations for your future self, how would you describe the gendered position you are forming and moving toward?

**(4) Implicit Norms & Layer Dynamics**

**Assumptions of Connection:** Map the gendered connections you take for granted—for example: being classified as a particular gender → a particular appearance / body / personality / role / desire / relationship form. Write down as many habitual pathways in which A automatically implies B as you can, and consider how those connections were formed.

**Assumption of Alignment:** Have you ever been required, by yourself or others, to make your appearance, body, identity, social role, and institutional gender consistent and free of contradiction? When the layers do not align, are you more inclined to adjust yourself to fit the norm, or to let them remain separate without forcing unity?

**Assumption of Fixity:** Do you assume that gender should remain stable? When your gendered feelings, expression, or identity change, do you treat this as a temporary deviation, or can gender itself remain fluid and change over time?

**Gender Forces:** Looking back on your experiences, how have the forces of gender norms typically acted upon you? Consider being watched and read by others, being required to perform gender, internalizing gendered bodily habits, being assigned and recognized by institutions, and experiencing pressure, correction, or punishment when departing from norms. Identify where these forces come from, which layer of gender they act upon, and whether they reinforce or conflict with one another.
