# A Finger Points, a Life Assigned

## A Proposal to Translate *Sex* as 性指 (*xingzhi*)

The 指 (*zhi*) in 性指 (*xingzhi*) is like a finger making the lightest pointing gesture. With that single gesture, a child’s position is determined. One pointing gesture, and a child is assigned for life.

I hope this term can serve as a small act of resistance. When formulations such as “biological sex” bear down on us, perhaps it can introduce a slight loosening—something gently parodic that unsettles their weight.

The Chinese translation of the conceptual pair *sex–gender* has long been unsatisfactory. The dominant rendering, 生理性别–社会性别—literally, “biological sex–social gender”—presupposes a supposedly “natural” body. Yet the very notion of the “biological” has been naturalized by obscuring the processes of gender assignment and institutional coercion. This translation reproduces linguistic violence in the name of so-called “biological sex” and can readily be interpreted through the logic of biological determinism, even when that logic remains unstated. Within queer communities, it has already become an offensive expression.

Regrettably, even in the Simplified Chinese translation of Judith Butler’s recent book *Who’s Afraid of Gender?*, the phrase 生理性别—“biological sex,” one of the very formulations Butler most forcefully opposes—appears throughout the book.

When a translation represents only one position within a field and generates internal tension within the linguistic practices of other schools of thought, then, at the very least, respect for the thinker and sensitivity to the discursive environment should prevent us from introducing that thinker through a conceptual vocabulary that contradicts their work.

Under the influence of postmodern feminism, the concept of “assigned gender” has gained recognition among some queer people and gender-studies scholars. Yet the translation “biological sex” has remained almost entirely untouched. How can academia collude with the unexamined common sense of mainstream and conservative culture?

The alternative rendering 性–性别 alleviates some of these problems, but the single character 性 creates difficulties of its own. In running prose, it produces ambiguity: does it mean a quality, a form, an innate nature, sexual activity, a sex characteristic, or gender? As a one-character term, 性 also creates problems of parsing and sentence rhythm.

Consider Julia Serano’s concept of “subconscious sex.” Translating it as 潜意识性 (*qianyishi xing*) produces an obscure and indeterminate expression—as though 性 were merely being used as another nominalizing suffix, indistinguishable from the countless “xxxx-性” formations scattered throughout Chinese academic writing.

In ordinary social life, moreover, administrative documents generally label the relevant field as either *sex* or 性别. The single character 性 has never appeared on identity documents as the name of such a field.

The use of 性 to translate *sex* was also influenced by Japanese translation practices and by twentieth-century naturalistic and scientistic understandings of the two sexes. The character therefore carries connotations of “innate nature” and “essential nature,” occupying the semantic position found in the classical phrase 人之初，性本善—“At the beginning of human life, human nature is good.” This may once have been an ingenious translation, but it has left traces of essentialism within the language.

Rendering *sexed* in Chinese as 性化 also risks confusion with the Chinese term for “sexualization,” meaning the treatment of someone as a sexual object.

I therefore propose a new pair of translations:

> *sex* — 性指 (*xingzhi*)
>
> *gender* — 性别 (*xingbie*)

This is only a preliminary proposal, offered in the hope of inviting better alternatives. Yet scholars within the academic establishment have been unable—or perhaps unwilling—to assume responsibility for this problem. We have therefore reached a historical moment at which the accumulated difficulties can no longer be ignored and must be placed explicitly on the agenda.

The inspiration for this short essay came from the interpretive possibilities of the slash in the alternative rendering 性/别.

Functionally, 性指 and 性别 form a balanced pair of two-character translations. They avoid the rhythmic awkwardness of the single character 性 and the confusion created when 性 resembles a suffix equivalent to “-ness.” They also slightly dilute and defamiliarize the association between 性 and “innate nature.”

The difficulty of translating *sex* once even made me consider a purely phonetic rendering such as 赛克斯 (*saikesi*). In that sense, the novelty and defamiliarizing effect of 性指 are entirely deliberate.

## 1. 性指 contains the idea of assignment

性指 explicitly evokes 性指派—sex/gender assignment—and foregrounds the institutional processes of designation and coercion. It carries a warning against the naturalization of sex/gender assignment and against the discourse of the “biological.”

At present, 指派性别 is generally used only when the English word *assigned* is explicitly present. When *sex* appears without *assigned*, it is rarely translated through the language of assignment. Rendering *sex* as 性指, while translating *assigned sex/gender* as 指派性别, would offer queer feminism an effective and elegant conceptual pair.

It would re-anchor *sex* in a dynamic and agentive social act: 指, the act of pointing, designating, and assigning. Each use of the term would compel us to ask:

Who is doing the pointing?

By what standards do they point?

What consequences follow from this act of designation?

## 2. 性指 can also evoke biomarkers and clinical signs

性指 can also be heard as a condensation of 性指标 and 性指征: the biomarkers and clinical signs through which bodies are medically observed, measured, and classified. This preserves the bodily dimension of *sex* as far as possible while remaining committed to anti-essentialism.

Biomarkers and clinical signs do not simply present themselves outside discourse. What counts as a relevant bodily marker, how it is measured, and how it is interpreted are all shaped through medical systems of knowledge. Treating *sex* as a shifting configuration of bodily markers avoids the linguistic trap in which bodily traits are assumed to be necessarily inborn and immutable. It preserves their variability and capacity for transformation while more clearly uncoupling them from *gender*.

In this sense, the body remains present—not as a rigid biological essence, but as a flexible materiality.

## 3. 指 is also a pointing finger

The character 指 in 性指 resembles a finger making the lightest pointing gesture. With that gesture, a child is placed in a position that may constrain and regulate them for the rest of their life.

One pointing gesture, and a child is assigned for life.

For those who need this translation, I hope the word itself can be treated as a small piece of poetic art. Each time it is used, perhaps it can sweep away some of the misery produced when the language of the so-called “biological” is wielded against us—and offer, in its place, a small charge of queer, parodic energy.
