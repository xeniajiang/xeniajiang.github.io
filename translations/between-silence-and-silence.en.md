# Between Silence and Silence
## Lesbian Subjectivity and Micro-Resistance in *Shanghai Lalas*


## I. “Small-Town Girls” and Double Silence

“The end of withering is the courage to bloom; that is the predicament I gladly accept.” This is a tender yet resilient line written by the musician Qi Zitan in her song “Small-Town Girl.” She portrays a woman trapped within the cultural logic of the “small town”: constrained by stereotypical gender expectations, her subjectivity ignored and excluded, she finds it difficult to break free from a predetermined course of life; yet within it, she carries a clean and unadulterated erotic desire and vitality, maneuvering and growing at once. I would like to borrow one of the song’s lines to open my discussion of Shanghai Lalas: between silence and silence.

Through detailed interviews and participant observation conducted among Shanghai lalas between 2005 and 2011, Lucetta Yip Lo Kam offers a vivid portrayal of a “double silence across the public and private spheres.” On the one hand, silence in the public sphere arises from the state’s construction and disciplining of tongzhi images. Although gay men, following decriminalization and depathologization, were gradually incorporated into narratives of public health and the scientization of sexuality, lesbians were further marginalized and rendered invisible because of judgments concerning their “invisible sexual practices,” their “unrecognized sexual desire,” and their supposed lack of threat to the social order. They could scarcely enter mainstream public discourse and were long treated as “a problem that did not count as a problem.”

On the other hand, silence in the private sphere arises from the normative logic of what is called “tolerance through silence” within family and cultural structures. Such “tolerance” often appears in the name of filial piety and face, but in practice constitutes an institutional arrangement marked by compulsory reciprocity. In a context where marriage remains the only legitimate path through which women are recognized as adults, lalas frequently become trapped in invisible relations of violence and are compelled, through internalized self-reproach, to participate in the reproduction of heterosexual norms.

The small town can be understood as a condition, a cultural logic, and a mechanism through which society operates. Lalas living in Shanghai may have moved there from hometowns where their private lives were closely monitored, seeking a small corner in which to practice a different way of life; or they may be native Shanghainese who, precisely because they already live in Shanghai, have lost the possibility of leaving their families in search of freedom. Shanghai provides only partial shelter through community. In many other respects, for people with diverse sexual and gender identities, it remains a “small town” in which autonomy is difficult to attain, while the logics of face, kinship, compulsory heterosexuality, and stigmatization continue to operate in much the same way. In my view, the predicament of lalas is not solely that of sexually and gender-diverse people. It reflects precisely the shared predicament of young women in contemporary Chinese cities: the denial of sexual subjectivity, the lack of freedom in geographical mobility, compulsory heterosexual marriage and the stigmatization of singlehood, “care” based on ableist assumptions, and the exploration of the self within the narrow spaces left by kinship and face culture. Lalas’ efforts to pursue same-sex erotic practices constitute only one form of women’s search for liberation in the domain of sexuality; in other sexual arrangements, as well as in fields unrelated to sexuality, the predicaments faced by young urban women in China have strikingly similar connections and roots.

“Between silence and silence,” meanwhile, foregrounds the position occupied by the subject and carries a strong perspective of subjective agency. As the stories recorded in the book show, lalas repeatedly maneuver between the double silence of the public and private spheres, as well as among other mechanisms of exclusion that go beyond silence, developing a series of strategies shaped by local cultural conditions, creating new possibilities for disrupting heteronormative family models, and producing certain new tongzhi counter-spaces.


## II. The Public Sphere: Lalas—A Problem That Does Not Count as a Problem

The construction of tongzhi images in public discourse often shapes how the wider public understands tongzhi people and even how tongzhi people internalize perceptions of themselves. As a macro-level and diffuse social atmosphere, it profoundly affects the circumstances of Shanghai lalas and the strategies through which they respond. For a long time, lalas occupied the position of “a problem that did not count as a problem” within public discourse. Following Kam’s perspective, we can trace the combined legal, medical, public-health, and academic forces through which this position was formed.

Tongzhi is a localized identity produced through history, whereas local discourse often politicizes it into the category of “the homosexual.” The term lala derives from the Taiwanese transliteration lazi (les) for lesbian and, after being reworked by local communities, became an identity label for female tongzhi people in mainland China. Before the end of the 1990s, homosexuals were consistently treated in public discourse as objects of pathologization and criminalization, classified as a form of “sexual perversion,” while gay men were often punished under the broadly interpretable crime of “hooliganism” in the Criminal Law. As subordinate subjects within the gender hierarchy, women’s “abnormal” sexual conduct was regarded as posing little threat to the mainstream social order. This meant that lesbians did not become targets of prosecution under the crime of hooliganism; while this left a certain amount of space for erotic practice, it also placed lalas at the margins of public discourse and rendered them invisible from the outset.

Since the late 1990s, local modes of controlling tongzhi people have changed. In the legal sphere, the crime of hooliganism was removed from the Criminal Law in 1997; in the medical sphere, homosexuality was removed from the classification of sexual perversions in 2001. Yet after decriminalization and depathologization, various forms of social control appeared in the name of the scientization of sexuality and public health, while prohibitions in parts of the medical and legal systems remained widespread. These forms of control continued earlier tendencies and produced two repeatedly recurring perspectives in Chinese public discourse on homosexuality: an overrepresentation of heterosexual standpoints and an excessive concentration on gay men.

In public discourse since the 1990s, heterosexual standpoints have occupied an overwhelming position, and only after the mid-2000s did some first-person accounts of same-sex relationships begin to appear. Tongzhi people were systematically constructed as the “Other.” Early public discourse took place within the framework of abnormal psychology. Scholars such as Ruan Fangfu and Zhang Beichuan, although often expressing sympathy for homosexuals in their work, still frequently remained within a repressive perspective. “The prevention and treatment of homosexuality” was a chapter commonly included in such publications. Medical knowledge is often an effective form of social control. Medical professionals play an important role within expert systems of social governance, and medical institutions are granted the authority to determine whether human behavior is normal and even whether it is lawful. Li Yinhe once reported the case of a gay man in the military who was punished for same-sex conduct as an act of sodomy; he avoided criminal punishment by going to a hospital and obtaining a diagnosis of “homosexual illness.”

Following depathologization, the discourses of public health and scientism became central to official advocacy. Here, “scientism” refers to an attitude that reduces complex human behavior to physiological determination and often ignores the multiple effects of social, cultural, and psychological factors. Gay men were constructed as subjects in need of management and guidance, while public discourse closely associated them with the transmission of AIDS, producing another mechanism of social control and stigma. As AIDS prevention became increasingly important within the official field of vision, more and more foundations were established in the name of public health, allowing gay male communities to gain access to a certain amount of resources and giving rise to new forms such as gay hotlines and websites. Throughout this process, however, lalas were once again rendered invisible because their sexual practices were difficult to imagine and were seen as posing no threat to public health. Early lala community activities suffered from a severe lack of resources and even had to borrow resources from gay male communities in order to establish their own telephone hotlines.

Scientistic discourse constructed homosexuality as innate and biologically essential. So-called scholars of psychiatry and sex psychology, acting as representatives of scientific authority, approached homosexual people from the standpoint of constructing an Other and devoted themselves to researching what “essential” physiological differences separated gay men from heterosexuals. Here I cite one representative psychiatric “authority,” referred to below as Dr. X. In view of the limitations of the period and the demands of the broader environment, this is not intended as a statement about his research position or personal prejudices, but as an attempt to reflect a representative discursive system of the 2000s that operated in the name of scientism and was saturated with prejudice.

Doctors and “sex-psychology experts” holding such views remain active throughout China today, and some of them have held the “power to diagnose” transgender people, thereby constituting institutional nodes with substantial real-world influence. Even in 2025, people with diverse sexual and gender identities, when seeking psychological counseling and mental-health services, still frequently find themselves in vulnerable situations in which they are pathologized and treated harshly. [To put it plainly six months later: I suffered.] The continuation of this discursive power is precisely a real manifestation of the ongoing discipline that persists after “depathologization.”

In media reports from 2004 and 2009, Dr. X was portrayed as a kind physician who had transcended prejudice and cared about tongzhi communities, and the reports attempted to attribute to him a certain idealistic spirit. “Because the causes of female homosexuality are too complicated, I decided to study male homosexuality.” Dr. X’s first-person account reflects the incomprehension and mystification produced at the time by the complete invisibility of lalas, as well as the reality of a severe imbalance in the allocation of resources.

“Research has found four major changes among male homosexuals: their androgen levels are only 65 percent of those of heterosexual men, while their estrogen levels are twice those of heterosexual men, although they retain sexual and reproductive functions. Their intellectual structure changes and becomes feminized to a certain degree, with linguistic and intuitive thinking abilities predominating and practical ability being relatively weak. Consequently, this functional structure means that most of them are concentrated in occupations perceived as feminine, such as fashion design, interior design, performance, beauty, and hairdressing. Their psychological responses resemble those of women; under psychological pressure, their first response is depression, hypochondria, and other manifestations of self-denial… They do not all lack knowledge of AIDS prevention; among them are people with the negative attitude that ‘at worst, I’ll die’ and who simply give up on themselves.” (Dr. X, 2004)

In the 2009 report, Dr. X’s conclusion had changed to: “The estrogen levels of gay men are three times those of normal people, which enables them to possess both men’s practical abilities and women’s linguistic and aesthetic abilities.” He also emphasized, in an extremely prejudiced manner, that “in the nearly one-hundred-year history of the Nobel Prize, not a single winner in the natural sciences has been homosexual.” From our present historical position, we can easily see the one-sided biological essentialism, the stereotypical assumptions about male and female abilities, the individualizing and stigmatizing reduction of psychological problems experienced by homosexual people under social pressure, and the process through which public discourse bound gay men to the transmission of AIDS.

The invisibility of lalas was also reflected in the difficulty early academic researchers encountered in gaining access to Chinese lala samples. Since specialized studies of homosexual populations first appeared in China in the 1990s, early research focused almost entirely on urban gay men, including the work of Zhang Beichuan, Li Yinhe and Wang Xiaobo, and Zhou Huashan. Ruan Fangfu, Zhang Beichuan, Li Yinhe, and Lisa Rofel all noted that it was difficult in China to locate and interview women in same-sex relationships.

It was not until 2003, when Tze-lan D. Sang published *The Emerging Lesbian: Female Same-Sex Desire in Modern China*, that the first monograph on Chinese lesbian culture appeared, tracing female same-sex desire in modern Chinese literary texts. During a brief period of fieldwork in Beijing in the 1990s, Sang encountered “spirited, confident lesbian-identified women in their early twenties who called themselves tongzhi,” and they had only just begun to communicate via the newly available internet.

Whether in law, public health, or academic writing, lalas fell into a position of invisibility because they were seen as posing little threat to the gender order or public health and because the sexual autonomy of unmarried women was not affirmed. They became “a problem that did not count as a problem.” In the public sphere, stigma and silence coexisted. While this left a certain amount of space for scattered lesbian relational practices, it also made it difficult for lala communities to contact one another during their early formation, left them short of funding, and even forced them to depend on the resources of gay male communities. Lalas consequently remained absent for a long time from mainstream imaginaries of sexual relationships and sexual practices.


## III. The Structural Predicament of the Private Sphere: The Web of Compulsory Heterosexual Marriage

Against the historical background of 2005–2010, although some parts of the world had already begun to experience the “deinstitutionalization” of marriage, in Chinese society it remained difficult for the overwhelming majority of young women to seek a life path outside heterosexual monogamous marriage. Following Reform and Opening, “non-orthodox heterosexual” sexual arrangements began to seek social acceptance. Singlehood, multiple sexual partners, cohabitation, extramarital affairs, and homosexuality gradually appeared in public discourse and were less constrained by ideological moral instruction than before Reform and Opening. Yet heterosexual marriage, as the only sexual arrangement to receive social acceptance, retained its dominant position. Marriage was regarded as an unquestionable and natural part of adult life.

Since the promulgation of the first Marriage Law in 1950, heterosexual monogamous marriage has remained the intimate-relational model enforced by the state, while state policies concerning resource allocation, social status, and community control have cooperated with and supported it. Medical discourse and systems of resource allocation worked together. The medical system used scientistic discourse to define heterosexual monogamous relationships as “biologically normal,” while the distribution of public resources systematically favored marital families: single women were marginalized not only in housing, welfare, and social evaluation, but were also symbolically positioned as “incomplete” or “immature” subjects.

By comparison, single men endured relatively less social pressure. This gender asymmetry reflects the gendered disciplinary logic underlying the institution of marriage. As Evans observed in 1997, mainstream discourse “leaves no alternatives for representations of a women’s sexual fulfillment except in the subject positions identified by the status of wife and mother”; in a context that naturalized monogamy as the only legitimate form of existence, women had little space in which to pursue sexual fulfillment and an independent life outside marriage.

As the work-unit system, which had previously exercised direct control over private life, gradually declined, the family became the most effective mechanism for supervising individual sexuality in everyday settings. Meanwhile, the emergence of tongzhi communities from the late 1990s onward, together with the fact that the need for same-sex relationships was no longer entirely suppressed, made it difficult for tongzhi people to fulfill the obligations of heterosexual marriage. Kam analyzes how the institutions of marriage and family became mechanisms through which society controlled lala life. Gender roles assigned by traditional culture, filial piety and lineage, and considerations of “face” all became major obstacles, producing a detailed portrayal of the private-life predicaments faced by lalas caught between compulsory heterosexual marriage and same-sex relationships.

Women generally face marital discipline at an earlier age, while their ability to evade or postpone marriage is also clearly restricted. Women possess less freedom of movement than men, and the space available to them for leaving home or living independently in another city before marriage is constrained, making it difficult in everyday practice to escape family supervision. For women from central cities such as Shanghai, this predicament is especially pronounced: compared with women from rural areas or economically underdeveloped regions, they lack a socially legitimate reason to migrate and establish themselves independently and find it more difficult to persuade their families to accept living alone or delaying marriage.

Inequality within the economic structure further intensifies this predicament. As marketization deepened, the income gap between men and women continued to widen, greatly reducing women’s possibilities for achieving economic independence outside marriage. Under these structural constraints, marriage instead became an important route through which some socially disadvantaged women obtained security and social recognition, and was even compelled to serve as a “strategy of upward mobility.”

In interviews, Shanghai lalas spoke about their awareness of marital pressure and the multiple difficulties involved in escaping the constraints of marriage. Beyond external pressures such as being urged to marry and having blind dates arranged for them, the mechanisms through which marital pressure became internalized were also made visible. Marriage as a symbol of female adulthood, marriage as a sign of responsibility toward family and society, and the occupation by marital relationships of every available image of a “model life” constituted three major mechanisms through which marital pressure was internalized.

Shu, an unmarried woman in her twenties, described the relationship among marriage, society, and family responsibility in the following terms:

> “Since marriage is not a simple matter of love, you need to consider many things, such as family [and] society … A person’s marital status can affect society, and a marriage can therefore affect things that one might not have thought would matter before. It can also well provide grounds for someone to attack you. And this will make your parents worry.”

Ying, who met her girlfriend before entering marriage, described the pressure and confusion produced by the absence of models for a life outside marriage:

> “I had never thought of marriage as optional. I felt that everyone must get married … I didn’t have any point of reference. I felt that everyone had to walk this path. Maybe at that time I thought I was just a bit different from others. But still you cannot upset the normal social order just because you’re different or special. You still have to bear responsibilities for your family, for your parents. I didn’t see how I could have the ability to challenge [all this]. At that time, both of us (Ying and her girlfriend) never thought about [not getting married]. It didn’t seem like an issue. It didn’t feel like a question worth serious consideration because it’s simply unimaginable to consider its possibility.”

Coral, a married woman in her thirties, described marriage as a collective matter:

> “If you have never gotten married, you probably won’t know what it feels like to have the weight of marriage on your shoulders, or what it really means. If I put it simply as responsibility, you might wonder what it means by responsibility. You might feel it’s an abstract idea. But actually, marriage is not just between two persons; it involves a lot more people, way more than the two persons. You’re talking about families, even friends, colleagues. You won’t understand—as a person who hasn’t gotten married before, you won’t really understand it. You might be able to understand my reasoning, or sympathize with me. But there are other feelings that you have no idea of.”

The story of Ying and her girlfriend carries a sense of complete individual powerlessness beneath structure. It is a cross-section of life in which “great joy is false, while great sorrow is real.” She emphasized that she had never previously imagined the possibility of any way of life outside marriage. Marriage was not a choice but a life process:

> “I think if we had had access to the Internet at that time, we might have gotten together; we might have stayed together and overcome many obstacles. You basically won’t think of living this kind of life. You feel there is no future for this. You think that no one would embark on this road or that this road is impossible to begin with. You feel that everyone should get married … I remember the night before my wedding, [my girlfriend] went with me and stayed at a hotel. There were many relatives at my home, and it gave me an excuse to stay out in order to get a good rest. So she stayed with me at a hotel room. The next morning, I had to return home to put my makeup on and to prepare for the wedding. It was really … together we were crying and crying till four in the morning. That was the night before my wedding.”

## IV. The Micro-Level Mechanisms of the Private Sphere: Single Women and the Violence of “Good Intentions”

Society subjects single women to surveillance and devaluation packaged as goodwill and care. Public opinion has long stigmatized unmarried women, creating a double burden of stigma for lalas. Behind the discourse of the “abnormal woman” lies a denial of young women as independent sexual subjects. Women who depart from mainstream patterns are subjected not only to formal or informal punishment, but may also be classified as sexual deviants and regarded as threats to social order and morality. Older unmarried women and their families alike become objects of other people’s scrutiny, while repeated inquiries that appear well intentioned carry an implicit suspicion that the woman may be physically or psychologically unhealthy.

The “old girl” became a fixed negative stereotype. In Shanghai, the image of the “old girl” associated older unmarried women with unattractive appearances, poor interpersonal relationships and health, and defects of individual character. Marital status was understood as closely connected to a person’s “inner essence,” and this stigma could even implicate her parents.

Chris, one of the interviewees, described herself as someone who loved freedom and was unsuited to family life:

> “I’m not going to hurt others, but I also don’t want myself to get hurt. This is why even if I’m unable to find a loving relationship, I wouldn’t mind—I’d rather be single. Yet this creates more pressure for me, because it is harder for families and friends to accept my decision. They think that in any case I should get married, be settled, and have a family. I told them that I am not suitable for this kind of life because I prefer freedom and don’t want to be restrained in any form. I have told them before. Of course I did not tell my parents that I wanted to be single. I think there’s no way they would accept it. I told my colleagues and friends that I wanted to be single, and they were so shocked! They said, ‘How can you!’ and immediately told me not to think this way.”

Ling, a lala in a contract marriage with a gay man, commonly known in Chinese queer communities as *xinghun*, tried to explain why parents became distressed when their daughter chose to remain single, and why people believed themselves entitled to interfere in a single woman’s private life:

> “A single woman usually gives others the impression of being unfortunate. Why? Who cares if she’s a career woman? You would still think that her love life is less than perfect. If not for the simple reason of not having a man who loves her—or even, not having a woman who loves her—then she would never choose to spend life alone. She is single because she cannot find a wonderful and harmonious relationship, one that satisfies her needs. Why would she be single if she can have a good relationship? It doesn’t matter if she’s career-driven. Even a workaholic won’t be like that. It is still the power of social pressure. Parents would feel that their child is by herself, and other outsiders would come up with different sorts of reasons—and then the pressure becomes too much to bear … Also, on many occasions you become the target of much concern if you aren’t married. Someone might say to you, is your daughter still unmarried, or, does she have a boyfriend? Or something like that. If you’re not married, you’ll always be the target of this kind of concern. Once you’re married, people leave you alone.”

The mechanism of this stigma can be summarized roughly as follows. First, unmarried women are defined as immature and unwilling to assume social responsibility, while women are prescribed as necessarily requiring a “destination,” and as needing emotional care in order to live and to make life “beautiful” and “harmonious.” This singular and crude prescription in fact constitutes a deprivation of women’s individuality. Because there is only one standard path, any action that fails to conform to that path and its norms is regarded as a failure and attributed to defects in the individual, or even to deficiencies in her family circumstances, resources, and education. “Helping single women escape loneliness” then becomes a “good deed” that accommodates the moral discourse of society, and the “moral” force that oversteps the boundaries of private life is used to carry out pervasive external interference. Within this institutionalized form of gentle violence, the language of social “good intentions” and “care” becomes a medium of discipline, causing women, in a process in which they appear to be cared for while actually being deprived, gradually to lose the legitimate capacity to imagine forms of life for themselves.

This reminds me of the different interpretations of cohabitation around the world against the background of the deinstitutionalization of marriage. The development of cohabitation has passed through four stages: from marginality, to a premarital transition, to an alternative to marriage, and finally to a condition indistinguishable from marriage. In some societies, this is regarded as an expression of changing values and individual autonomy; in other contexts, cohabitation remains marked by structural disadvantage. As revealed by the “pattern of disadvantage” (POD) proposed by Perelli-Harris in 2011, cohabitation among some groups is not a free choice, but a compelled alternative practice. By comparison, singlehood and nonmarital co-residence had gained no institutional or cultural legitimacy. They more closely resembled a condition of “forced singlehood” or “unnamed cohabitation”—a form of life sustained in the cracks between social stigma and moral discipline. This condition continually pushed lalas, and women more broadly, toward a passive mode of survival between “choosing” and “being chosen.”

In other words, this was not merely the private-life predicament of lalas. It was also the predicament shared at the time by every young single woman: the denial of sexual subjectivity, compulsory heterosexual marriage, the stigma of singlehood, and restricted mobility. To escape these conditions to any degree, economic independence became almost the only available strategy; yet the gender discrimination embedded within the market economy made economic independence exceptionally difficult. An image of womanhood as incomplete, dependent, passive, and longing for a “destination” was continually reproduced and reinforced through discourse, embedded in the cognition of every individual, and used to restrict the space within which women could imagine ways of living.


## V. Between the Public and the Private: The Culture of “Tolerance Through Silence” and the Image of the “Good Lala”

Is Chinese cultural tradition more tolerant toward tongzhi people? This claim frequently appears in nationalist texts that set the “tradition of tolerance” in Chinese culture against the forces of violent homophobia in the West, using this contrast to resist Western societies’ imagination of China as homophobic. Within Kam’s analytical framework, however, this “tolerance” is not genuine inclusion, but a force of regulation that takes silence as its form. Within the family structure, it operates through “wordless acceptance” and the principle that “not mentioning something is itself tolerance,” becoming an effective discourse through which sexual discipline is allowed to continue. It belongs to the same lineage as other, more directly visible forms of homophobia. Its consequence is the formation in contemporary China of a homonormative order centered on the “homosexual who can be tolerated.”

Regulation through silence is a force of erasure, causing tongzhi people to be completely hidden from public view and silenced. Through the traditional discourse of “filial piety” and the custom of “caring about face,” families establish and reinforce compulsory relations of reciprocal love and obligation between parents and children. Within traditional discourse, collective intervention by parents and relatives in an individual’s marriage is justified; marriage is regarded as an individual responsibility toward family and society; and nonnormative sexual subjects become destroyers of surface harmony and “face,” and therefore occupy a disadvantaged position within discourse.

Even when tongzhi people obtain limited room for agency through strategic performance or concealment, they must still preserve the surface harmony of family and kinship relations and cannot openly challenge this order. They are therefore subjected to pressure from every direction in their erotic expression, gender and sexual expression, and the public recognition of their relationships. This apparently tolerant culture also produces a micro-level social-psychological mechanism—internalized guilt. Under the force of guilt, tongzhi subjects themselves actively participate in practices that maintain the compulsory heterosexual order.

May, one of the interviewees, had a profound awareness of the erased voices and unseen suffering of tongzhi people:

> “It’s probably good that China and its culture are not extreme. After all, it is a patriarchal society and no one is particularly sensitive to the oppression experienced by lesbians or gay men. Everyone seems to lead a stable life. Many gay men and lesbians proceed to get married when it’s time to do so. One’s pain and suffering, one’s desolation—all is stashed away in a hidden corner, never to be exposed under the bright sunlight. Everyone thinks if you can’t see it then it doesn’t exist. That’s why no one cares about these issues much; these issues only come up gradually and occasionally, only if some tongzhi bar got too racy and got shut down, or if two persons went an extra mile to get married—just like that, just another piece of news coverage. People don’t really care much about the things that really happen among us [lalas]. Basically, I think standards are still quite relaxed. If you can manage your own feelings and find your own path, you won’t run into much of a problem. At least you won’t have the feeling of fearing for your life which you sometimes get living in a foreign country.”

May’s description of China and its culture as “not extreme” reveals a cultural logic of compromise and indirectness collectively maintained by society, one that sustains the surface harmony of “tolerance through silence.” The apparent stability of everyday life, meanwhile, implies strategic concealment and self-suppression under discipline; and the “hidden corner” and the belief that “if you can’t see it then it doesn’t exist” make even clearer that tongzhi people have never truly been included, but have instead been systematically ignored and erased. This moderated harmony is precisely the cultural form taken by silent violence—it does not appear as conflict, yet it continuously produces exclusion without a voice.

Lalas were thrown into a silent competition to become “good lalas,” with conditional tolerance as the prize. When this culture of silence encountered the “politics of public correctness” in the public sphere, tolerance was transformed into a mechanism of compliance that encouraged performative behavior, and “normalization” became the central disciplinary discourse connecting the public and private spheres. For many years, society had institutionally stigmatized tongzhi people, and the discourse of “normalization” can be understood as a response to that stigmatization. Experts who promoted what was “scientific” and “correct,” consciously or unconsciously, constructed a larger project of sexual morality: in a neoliberalized and commodified society, they helped build a new sexual morality whose primary purpose, as Lisa Rofel wrote in 2007, was to establish “what counts as an appropriate homosexual.”

In this process, Warner’s concept of “heteronormativity” and Duggan’s account of “homonormativity” became intertwined: the former maintained the universality of heterosexual norms, while the latter reconsolidated them under an apparently progressive form. Tongzhi people were required to perform more “correctly” in public, becoming law-abiding citizens, economically contributing members of society, filial children, and “model homosexuals.” Such “correct performance” was regarded not only as a means of producing harmony within the family, but also as a prerequisite for winning social acceptance.

Scholars have disagreed over the mechanisms and consequences of “tolerance through silence.” Zhou Huashan argued in 2000 that homosexual people used a series of strategies compatible with Chinese culture to respond to the silent oppression of family and society, which he summarized as nonconfrontational, not centered on sexuality, not based on a heterosexual/homosexual division, and guided by traditional Chinese values. Li Yinhe argued in 2002 that the principal mode through which homosexual subjects were oppressed locally was not the “ruthless persecution and extreme hostility” found in Western societies, but “ignorance and prejudice in the mainstream society.” She used this to explain why many local homosexual subjects adopted nonconfrontational strategies of evading reality. To some extent, these arguments reflected the relationship between the conditions of homosexual life and the strategies of agency available at the time, emphasizing differences between China and the West; yet they also carried the implication of essentializing a “Chinese tradition of tolerance.”

Liu Jen-peng and Ding Naifei pointed out in 2007 the problem of essentializing a “Chinese tradition of tolerance.” Their article exposed the internal violence imposed on homosexual people through tolerance enacted by silence, and argued that Zhou Huashan’s so-called “poetic of reticence” was itself a form of violence that deprived homosexual subjects of discursive authority and visibility. Wendy Brown argued in 2006 that “tolerance” depoliticizes and individualizes political issues, replacing a fundamental challenge to the relevant logic with the “failure” of an individual to comply with collective norms. This logic draws a boundary between what “should be tolerated” and what “cannot be tolerated.”

For Brown, tolerance “involves two kinds of boundary drawing and a practice of licensing.” The first establishes “spatial boundaries of dominion and relevance”: who may speak and rule, and which issues may count as publicly significant. The second establishes “moral boundaries about what can and cannot be accommodated within this domain.” Together, these boundaries determine who possesses discursive authority and whose life can count as a public matter.

Within Chinese culture, the practice of granting permission to tongzhi people is conditional. Tolerance resembles an exchange conducted under domination. Its conditions can be summarized approximately as follows: obey the rule of silence within the family and internalize shame toward oneself. When nonnormative sexual subjects express anything concerning sexuality within the family, self-humiliation is the only emotion permitted to appear, emphasizing their failure to preserve family face and to pursue marriage and reproduction. In fact, because of the central role played by the family in regulating sexual practice, we should regard this pressure of compulsory compliance and internalized self-humiliation as a crucial component of the “minority stress” borne by local homosexual populations.

Kam refers collectively to this tongzhi political background and set of practices spanning the public and private spheres as **the politics of public correctness**, and uses “a smile on the surface” to describe the efforts of some lalas to pursue public correctness. We must also recognize that the politics of public correctness and the homonormative order are discriminatory. They use a single standard to judge all tongzhi individuals situated differently across gender and sexual identity, class, region, age, and other intersecting positions, even though different individuals vary greatly in their ability to adapt to this standard, in the resources available to them, and in the extent to which they are willing to accommodate it. The result was what might be called **a hierarchy of complicity** within queer communities, in which access to recognition was distributed according to uneven resources and different degrees of compliance. Dudu, a pseudonymous participant in a *xinghun*, offered an apt annotation in the lala magazine *Les+*:

> “A fake marriage is a promise between ourselves and our families. On the surface we keep a smile, but the sacrifices we make are more than anyone can imagine.”




## VI. Individual Agency: Queer Subject Strategies within the Family

Within this familial order of “tolerance through silence,” lalas’ everyday practices did not cease; resistance unfolded through subtle strategies of daily life. The period from 2005 to 2011 was one in which old and new ideas about the private sphere were intertwined, and different families varied greatly in the degree of support they offered children with nonnormative sexual or gender identities. Wu Youjian was a mother from Guangzhou. In 2007, she publicly expressed support for her gay son in the media and received praise from some internet users. The report about her was followed by an online survey in which more than 70 percent of participating parents said that they could accept their child being homosexual (N = 3,977).

Samples in this kind of online survey are usually composed of readers who already hold relatively open attitudes toward tongzhi issues. Bias enters both through who chooses to read the report and through who selects themselves into the survey, making the results excessively positive. Most of the Shanghai lala interviewees in Kam’s fieldwork had received no support from their parents regarding their sexual or gender identities, and some had never even imagined that parental support might be possible.

Living within unsupportive family environments, Shanghai lalas developed multiple forms of micro-resistance in order to gain some room in which to practice lesbian relationships.

The first kind of practice concerned the balance between concealment and coming out. Pretending to be heterosexual was a common temporary expedient. Coming out was not regarded as a responsibility; at certain moments, abruptly coming out was even regarded within the community as irresponsible. Strategic coming out, striving to improve one’s economic achievements, and attempting to leave home became several typical ways of trying to escape control.

The second kind of practice concerned married lalas who had been forced into heterosexual marriage and how they balanced the tension between heterosexual marriage and same-sex relationships. This included secret double lives as well as frank disclosure and negotiation.

The third kind of practice displayed the creativity that queer life could generate under real-world constraints. Even when these arrangements remained immature, unstable, or difficult to define, they still opened up a certain space for queer resistance. *Xinghun* and queer families were representative emerging practices at the time.

The balance between concealment and coming out concerned lalas’ carefully considered efforts to secure recognition. Concealment usually required some means of pretending to be heterosexual. When the conditions for coming out were not yet mature, they used heterosexual performance to temporarily dispel their parents’ concerns and preserve the superficial order of peace within the family. Under pressure from family-arranged blind dates, several interviewees chose to attend in order to avoid an obvious breach of “courtesy.” At times, they sensed genuine concern for them in the family’s pressure to marry and regarded attending blind dates as part of their responsibility as younger family members, as well as “a way of showing gratitude to the family.” For the sake of a blind date, even a **T**—a locally specific lala identity associated with masculine presentation and relational positioning—might temporarily shift her gender expression toward a conventionally feminine image. Sometimes this was merely an expedient measure; at other times, it also signaled the individual’s despair at the prospect of resisting compulsory heterosexual marriage. Concealment did not always lead toward a mature opportunity to come out.

When marriage was regarded as part of an individual’s responsibility to family and society, coming out was no longer understood as a responsibility to treat one’s family honestly. On the contrary, because an abrupt coming out might damage the family’s surface harmony, “face,” and kinship relations, some people regarded it as an irresponsible, impulsive, and immature act. Coming out was often an unmistakably strategic action undertaken only after careful consideration. There was an unspoken “two-step” method of coming out: first appear as a “model” member of society, and only afterward come out as a sexual individual who was “less than ideal.” This idea—that compliance with social norms should serve as the precondition for tongzhi survival—directly echoed the mainstream discourse through which sexual minorities were socially managed and fell into the logic of normalization and the politics of public correctness.

As an experienced volunteer answering calls on a lala hotline, May was skeptical about the necessity of coming out:

> “Although coming out is a very admirable behaviour, it also depends on the situation. It’s not like you are going to be a martyr. (Interviewer: Will you say so when you are on the hotline?) Yes, I tell callers that I do not encourage one to come out. This is a very serious matter, one that requires serious consideration, especially if your conditions don’t allow you to come out. It’s about being realistic. I often tell them directly in a stark, naked way, can you afford to come out?”

Waiting until one’s economic position had improved before coming out was a typical practice. On the one hand, lalas who had internalized guilt might feel driven to compensate their families through worldly success, using achievements that could be exchanged for “face” to make up for the family’s “loss” of being unable to enjoy grandchildren. On the other hand, escaping compulsory family control usually required sufficient economic power. For lalas living in Shanghai, because Shanghai was already one of the most developed cities in the country, it was difficult to use career development as a reason to move to another city. Leaving home often required independently purchasing property. Physical separation from the family of origin sometimes also became a means of preserving family “face” and seeking compromise. For example, the interviewee Jenny planned to leave China with her girlfriend in order to reduce her parents’ intense reaction to their same-sex relationship, a reaction rooted in their fear of “losing face.”

Strategic coming out sometimes took the form of “gradual, soft coming out.” The interviewee Flora first pretended to show her parents a photograph of herself and her girlfriend casually, and then looked for an opportunity to invite her girlfriend home to have dinner with them. Before living with her girlfriend and making the relationship public, she hoped to give her parents time to prepare themselves psychologically. Some younger interviewees were more proactive in discussing sexual orientation with their parents. Moon, for example, used the then-popular television competition *Super Girl* as an opportunity to tell her mother. Her mother liked Li Yuchun very much and had seen media photographs of Li Yuchun kissing girls, as well as reports about her relationship with He Jie, yet none of this diminished her fondness for Li Yuchun.

Moon felt that the moment to come out had arrived:

> “I went on to say that there are plenty of girls like Li Yuchun in every city, every country, and throughout the world, but that a lot of people don’t get it. I said there are tens of millions in China, many in the city where I lived, and that there are even more in my university.”

She then had a sincere conversation with her mother. This act of coming out succeeded, and her mother accepted her. Drawing on a family member’s interests, beliefs, or shared cultural references can create openings for situational empathy during coming out.

Compulsory heterosexual marriage placed lalas within an apparently irreconcilable conflict between marriage and same-sex relationships. They had to face pressure from three directions at once: the family of origin, the marital family, and the same-sex relationship outside marriage. Individuals made different choices between secret double lives and open disclosure and negotiation, while entering a *xinghun* was also regarded as one possible strategy.

At the time, divorce affected the “face” of both families and was extremely difficult. Ying and Coral both left their hometowns and husbands to live in Shanghai, and geographical separation became central to maintaining a difficult balance between marriage and relationships outside marriage. Because divorce carried a high social cost, even after they had long been emotionally estranged from their husbands, they still had to perform the appearance of a “normal” marriage before others.

Mu, a woman in her early thirties, lived abroad with her girlfriend. Both of their husbands knew about their relationship. The geographical distance, together with the fact that their partners were women, made the husbands more willing to accept their extramarital relationships. Mu said:

> “What was his reaction? He was taken by surprise. It was like, he could write a story about his life. How could something like this happen to him? But since it’s happened, and the other party is a woman, her being of the same sex poses less of a threat to him. And with the distance between us, he came to accept it. He wouldn’t have if it had been a man. It was the same with her husband. He thinks two women can’t really do anything! (Laughs.) But her husband was really angry at first … really angry. How could his wife be involved in an affair? And later when he found out it’s with a woman, he came to accept it gradually.”

How much of this apparent acceptance depended on men’s refusal to recognize women as sexual subjects, or on their inability to imagine lesbian sex?

*Xinghun* occupies an unstable position between compliance and resistance. Several of Kam’s interviewees had already entered such marriages or planned to marry gay men. One major risk was that no one could anticipate all the problems that might arise after marriage, while the need to deceive parents and acquaintances over a long period also created a heavy moral burden.

Kam nevertheless argues that the creativity within *xinghun* deserves attention. *Xinghun* not only constructs a certain tongzhi counter-space, but also opens up new ways of life and new forms of queer family. Drawing on Judith Halberstam’s concept of “queer temporality,” Kam argues that queer time and space do not follow a heteronormative life-course sequence centered on marriage and reproduction. Queer temporality redefines social standards of success and maturity through failure, delay, detour, and interruption.

*Xinghun* opens up the possibility of new life narratives, family forms, intimate relationships, and tongzhi spaces; it is not merely a compromise with the mainstream paradigm of marriage.

During the same period, “lala families” composed of multiple lalas also appeared. Family members assigned “family roles” according to their own characteristics—for example, a T as the “father,” a **P**—a locally specific identity associated with feminine presentation and relational positioning—as the “mother,” together with a “daughter” and “female cousins” who frequently visited or lived with them. This reflects the creative practice through which lalas borrowed the role concepts of heterosexual families and established a queer order of life through parody. Queer-family practices carried strong elements of the “chosen family,” emphasizing family-like intimacy formed through voluntariness, consensus, and affection.

We can likewise understand *xinghun* as a parody of heterosexual marriage. It dissolves the distinction between the real and the false, exposes the performative components of heterosexual marriage, and thereby prompts a reconsideration of the institution itself. In much the same way that cross-dressing helped make the performativity of gender analytically visible, *xinghun* can expose the performative dimensions of heterosexual marriage. Of course, as the compulsory force of marriage has gradually declined, the necessity of *xinghun* has also become less pronounced than before.


## VII. Collective Agency: The Formation and Activities of Shanghai Lala Communities

The possibility that lalas might share a culture and a way of life, and even consolidate their identification with their own sexual and gender identities, was often formed through community interaction. As early as the late nineteenth century, Shanghai had legends of the “Mirror-Polishing Party.” They were said to be descendants of the “Ten Sisters,” a group founded several centuries earlier by a Buddhist nun in Chaozhou, Guangdong. The women lived together and resolutely protected their lesbian relationships. According to legend, the Mirror-Polishing Party lasted for approximately twenty years and attracted around twenty members. Three were mistresses of wealthy men, one was unmarried, and the remaining dozen or so were widows from affluent families. They were demonized as women who “used sexual skills to attract new members,” while “Mirror-Polishing Party” became a negative term of ridicule in everyday Shanghai speech.

From the end of the twentieth century to the beginning of the twenty-first, Shanghai’s lala community passed through a historical period from emergence to growing activity. Several broad forces enabled collective interaction among lalas to move from absence to existence, from impossibility to imaginability, and from invisibility to partial visibility. The first was the population mobility of the 1980s through the 2000s. Rural reform, the establishment of special economic zones, the relaxation of household-registration controls, employment opportunities created by private enterprises in the 1990s, and the introduction of the commercial housing market brought fundamental changes to the labor market. These developments were accompanied by a loosening of state control, the weakening of the work-unit system, and greater autonomy in private life. Newly acquired geographical mobility encouraged people to move from less-developed regions of the country to cities in search of work, and cities such as Shanghai gathered large numbers of migrant workers from across China. Increased population mobility and the emergence of commercial meeting places both helped tongzhi communities gather individuals who had previously remained scattered.

Second, as discussed in the section on the public sphere, modes of governance shifted: overt criminalization and pathologization receded, while regulation through scientific discourse, public health, normalization, and social guidance expanded. Sexual morality in large cities also changed significantly, as sexuality shifted from a social and collective orientation toward a private and individual one. Ruan Fangfu (1991) believed that the requirement of contraception under the family-planning policy unexpectedly liberated sexual behavior from an ideological framework that confined it to reproduction. During the 1990s, the sexual-health industry grew substantially. McMillan (2006) attributed this to the government’s conscious effort to create a scientific and civilized discourse on sexuality, as well as its attempt to regulate sexuality within the permitted bounds of the public interest. Within this discourse, sexual pleasure was associated with the bodily health of the population. Evans (1997) noted that, beginning in the early 1980s, substantial discussions of women’s sexual pleasure appeared in magazines for the first time. Sexual activity oriented toward private pleasure increasingly appeared alongside reproduction as a legitimate object of public discussion.

Against this background of changing sexual morality, people previously labeled “abnormal,” such as single women and tongzhi people, encountered more tolerant public attitudes. Urban single women working outside the work-unit system became more socially acceptable, and the stigma of singlehood was greatly reduced for economically independent women, even though it was not entirely eliminated. Changes in economic reality and sexual morality jointly brought about a relaxation of political and social control and became important conditions for the emergence of urban gay and lesbian communities. State intervention nevertheless remained constant, and because markets developed within state power rather than in opposition to it, communities remained extremely sensitive and vulnerable in the face of change.

As macro-level institutional and cultural structures gradually loosened, the existence of lalas began to find shelter in the cracks of the urban night. Their public presence was first sought, tested, and generated not in the political sphere, but within commercial and entertainment spaces. Lala bars were the most representative and active public social spaces for lesbians. Under the constraints of the economic and sociocultural environment, they displayed several recurring patterns: venues might be rented temporarily while the “core remained unchanged”; fixed venues frequently changed, relocated, or closed; spaces faced irregular regulatory interventions; and lala gatherings sometimes depended on the offline spaces of gay men.

Period of dependence: In 1998, a small group of lalas gathered at “Eighty Percent,” one of the earliest bars in the city center. Before 2000, many lalas met in gay male bars downtown, the most popular of which was Eddy’s 1924 near People’s Square. In the late 1990s, gay male bars and other little-known venues were the only public spaces in which lalas could meet and communicate offline.

Period of venue rental and independence: Regular lesbian parties began to appear in 2000. The most popular arrangement involved bar owners and party organizers cooperating to rent out the entire venue, usually on the busiest Friday and Saturday nights, and sharing revenue from tickets, drinks, and food orders. The 1088 bar, which began operating in the summer of 2002, was described as the first bar to follow this model, although the memoir discussed below suggests that this may not have been entirely accurate. Its events were nevertheless suspended in 2003. Even so, the reputation of “1088” made the term synonymous with the lala community, and an online website named after 1088 even appeared. At the end of 2004, the owner of 1088 sold the bar and became an organizer of “nomadic” lesbian parties in the city center.

Kam’s account abruptly ends here, while a memoir that has circulated to the present allows us to hear another voice—the voice of women who were there, who witnessed these events, and who lived in the narrow space between lights and regulation. In 2025, I unexpectedly found this long essay on Douban. It was first published on the Tianya forum in 2006 under the name “In Love with Memory.” In nearly nine thousand Chinese characters, the author looks back delicately on Shanghai’s lala world from 2001 to 2005, supplementing Kam’s writing with subjective experiences and emotional details that her account was unable to present.

The opening of the memoir is deeply affecting: just as I looked back at them in 2025, she, writing in 2006, was already looking back at the people of 2001–2005. It feels like the echo of a historical conversation nested inside another.

“I stopped at the intersection of Liyang Road. The doorway leaned beneath an orange-green light shining through it. It was now a Saturday in May 2005. The place had already lost its former appearance and its so-called prosperity. It was a declining tongzhi bar, and the letters ‘SUBHOUSE’ on its sign were badly designed, as though the place were some kind of space-capsule experience. Once, on Saturdays and Sundays, crowds of women gathered outside. They came for nothing else but the four large numbers—1088—written in dark red on the enormous sign that used to hang there.”

Recalling Eddy’s 1924, she describes its owner as a thin and kindly middle-aged gay man. At the time, 1924 gathered all the Shanghai lalas who could be gathered through the internet. Its sixty square meters, together with the corridor, seemed to represent the total number of visible Shanghai lalas at that time.

The bar was quiet on ordinary days. On weekends, “a curtain was drawn across the entrance. Only after enduring the screaming music inside, paying for a ticket, and entering would you discover the strange lighting mixed with a seductive atmosphere. The bar’s most famous dance track, MU-MA’s ‘Temptation,’ made you feel that you would not leave unless you had stripped off your own human skin.” She also mentions that, somewhat earlier, Eddy’s boyfriend operated another lala bar in Jinmao. This was an “ancient,” “Cretaceous-era” gathering place that Kam did not trace.

Eddy’s 1924 was subjected to a sudden regulatory raid. Beneath the memoirist’s understated writing lies a traumatic memory shared by an entire community. Recalling one day in March 2002, she writes: “Sometime after nine, the curtain was lifted by a sharp-looking man, and cries of ‘Everyone get down!’ rang through the bar street. In an instant, the music stopped, the crowd scattered and fled outside, and there were sounds of breaking glass and shouting. After more than ten minutes of chaos, we were all placed in the open space outside. At that moment, I saw a huge dark mass of lalas crouching there. I was astonished. Could such a narrow space as 1924 really have held so many people? Was it my illusion? But whatever the case, I understood one thing: the era of 1924 had ended.”

After 1924 closed, lalas were forced back into their homes. This period even became an important time of growth for lala websites, while the small downturn in offline activity did not last long. “With the fall of 1924, contenders rose across the entire city… By late spring and early summer, lalas suddenly did not know which venue to choose for the weekend.”

The 1088 bar opened in August 2002. She attributed its success to the fact that its organizers were “persistent and attentive.” Its event planning, online promotion, invitations, and other efforts were all highly attractive to customers and gave it a strong reputation within the lala community. From 2003 onward, however, undercurrents began to emerge within 1088. The 1088 online platform mentioned by Kam was in fact established by a commercially astute man who supported lala-related ventures. It sold commemorative merchandise and maintained a publicity team. Here we can see the early commercialization of identity politics under market mechanisms: within consumer culture, a shared minority identity and its symbols were transformed into consumable and commodified signs of identity.

An even more interesting memory concerns the fact that, in 2003, being tongzhi briefly “became a sign of fashion.” Twenty years later, the controversy over “underage trend-following lalas” produced by the increased visibility of lalas on short-video platforms became another echo. Increased visibility is always accompanied by attempts at exploration, yet regardless of where such exploration eventually leads, the expansion and growing youthfulness of a population may cause it to be interpreted through heterocentric norms as conformity, imitation, and falseness. At the same time, this controversy also reflects the new status of lala culture as a visible subcultural symbol.

Two famous bars underwent “transformations” that produced feelings of disappointment. In 2003, when 1924 was close to being forgotten, a married couple drew upon older lalas’ attachment to 1924 and organized rented events at its former location. The couple had returned from the United States and had redesigned the place to be “elegant” and “like a teahouse.” In August 2004, rumors spread that 1088 had “begun catering to the mainstream.” It was renamed “SUBHOUSE,” and lalas were “confined to a small area.” They faced the intrusion of men and mainstream customers and experienced “a constant discomfort of avoidance and constraint.” From then on, disappointment with 1088 began to grow. On New Year’s Eve between 2004 and 2005, the websites “Where Flowers Bloom” and “1088” attempted to preserve the position of the 1088 name within the lala community. “Blooming 1088” was established at the Magic bar in the Rose Square commercial street near Zhongshan Park. Through this action, online lala platforms performed the function of gathering resources for offline social assembly.

Independent lala bars began to appear. “In June 2004, a bar devoted entirely to lala business, the ‘Red Bar,’ unexpectedly opened opposite 1024.” “A T-shirt covered with the signatures of familiar lalas hung diagonally across the large window. Everything was red upon entering. The pure, proper redness made the name feel revolutionary and fitting. Red Bar was small, and a wooden staircase connected it to the second floor, creating a sense of mystery and anticipation.” Its two owners promoted it as a purely lala bar, causing customers to develop an extremely rapid sense of identification with the space. Unlike other venues that served mainstream customers during ordinary hours and only cleared the premises and displayed a lala sign at night, Red Bar “did red thoroughly: day or night, weekday or holiday, it always kept its own sign displayed.” Lala-controlled space had reached an unprecedented degree of institutional and commercial independence, achieving a form of “purity” in both economic and cultural terms despite numerous restrictions. Yet Red Bar was ultimately abandoned by its owner after a fight between two Ts, demonstrating the fragility of independent lala space.

The memoirist describes the influence of lala bars on her own lala identity in these terms: “Human by day, lala by night… I kept exploring the questions I had about myself and their answers. The bar possessed an endless attraction. Every week it pulled me toward the weekend. It was my only pleasure. My love for women changed from what had once been an accident into a necessity—the only possibility.” In the afterword, she raises another series of profound questions: “Why can’t we simply walk into an ordinary bar to relax, to be happy, or to indulge ourselves? Is it other people who reject us, or have we imprisoned ourselves?”

What this memoir presents is not merely a set of scattered personal memories, but a logic of community formation. It allows us to see that lala public life initially depended on gay male spaces and gradually searched for a place of its own through repeated relocation and regulation; the commercialized spaces of “1088” and “Red Bar” represented both the incorporation of minority groups into market logic and a strategy for seeking recognition within consumer culture; and those fragments of being “a person by day and a lala by night” reveal the everydayness and contradiction of identity—a mode of existence repeatedly negotiated between social norms and personal desire. When the memoirist asks why lalas cannot enter ordinary bars, she is also questioning the stability of the heterocentric order and the formation of self-confinement. More importantly, narration in the form of memory has itself become a practice of historical writing: through fragmented language and intense emotion, these women left behind an affective archive belonging specifically to female subjects for a history that had been neglected.


Online lala spaces emerged rapidly. The internet became a new refuge and also a kind of “virtual neighborhood,” carrying an unprecedented density of emotion. Before and during the mid-1990s, texts about homosexuality from Hong Kong and Taiwan circulated on a small scale only in cities where people could come into direct contact with foreigners. By the late 1990s, the internet began to spread in China, and information concerning homosexuality began to cross national borders. After 2000, independent lala websites appeared. Three representative Shanghai websites were Ala Island, Late Autumn Cottage, and Where Flowers Bloom. I was surprised to discover that Late Autumn Cottage remains in operation today, distributed across platforms both inside and outside China, and that it has preserved an older version of its webpage as a shared online memory for lalas.

Several of Kam’s interviewees emphasized that their earliest explorations of sexual and gender identity came from information on the internet and from online communities. In 2003, Moon searched Baidu for the Chinese term *nü tong*, a common abbreviation for “lesbian.” Websites and academic reports appeared. She learned concepts such as T, P, and “no distinction,” and applied them to her own relationship with another girl, thereby clarifying many concepts for herself. She then entered an underdeveloped website called “Where Dreams Begin,” and, with the guidance of a newly acquainted lala, found an online lala chatroom. She recalled:

> “Once I got in, I was like—wow! All of the people here are les; there are so many of them. I’m not alone in this; I’m not a freak. In fact, there are so many people like myself!”

Through this, she realized that her experience of secretly loving someone but being unable to confess was shared by many others. She came to believe that they were all “normal,” while the lives of some lalas in Beijing allowed her to “see hope in the distance.”

Offline community activities began to take shape in the early 2000s, including lesbian hotlines, volunteer work, and regularly organized salon gatherings. In June 2004, the first lesbian hotline, “Tongxin Hotline,” appeared. It depended on an earlier gay male hotline, recruited volunteers through tongzhi websites, and possessed a semi-official character. A former operator at Tongxin Hotline recalled that it received fewer calls from women than the gay male hotline received from men, and that many callers were not local Shanghai residents. One rule was “do not encourage callers to enter homosexuality.” The hotline adopted a position of “recognition without encouragement,” especially toward callers who were not economically independent.

In 2005, Tongxin Hotline suspended its operations. In 2006, the same group of female volunteers established a new hotline, the “800 Free Hotline,” with support from the Chi Heng Foundation in Hong Kong. It operated for two hours every Saturday afternoon. In June 2005, the grassroots lala organization Nü’ai began operating the first lala hotline without official or overseas affiliation. It opened every Friday evening and continued until late at night.

Nü’ai was the first grassroots lala organization. In 2005, lesbian groups from across the country gathered in Beijing for the first time, and the first lala magazine, Les+, was born. Beginning in 2007, the “Lala Camp” emerged and connected cultural and political exchange among local and overseas lala communities, becoming an important site for the production of discourse within tongzhi politics at the time. From hotlines to magazines, from salons to training camps, these efforts marked the leap of lala communities from “invisibility” to “self-organization”—a form of collective agency that moved from passive gathering toward active generation.

The first “Lala Camp” was held in Guangdong in 2007 as a three-day training camp. It later developed into an annual event, gathering organizers and scholars from Chinese lesbian communities in Hong Kong, Taiwan, North America, and other regions. It represented a historic moment of cross-regional connection and exchange, and each year it helped bring about the rapid emergence of lala groups in different cities.

From the late 1990s to the middle of the 2000s, the formation of Shanghai’s lala community was not a linear history of growth, but a social history that continually doubled back among political discipline, market logic, and emotional longing. The lights of bars, the usernames on websites, and the voices on hotlines were all small yet tenacious forms of “collective agency” within the city. Together, they constructed a subpolitical space generated outside state discourse and within women’s experience. Kam’s writing provides us with the framework of social structure, while “In Love with Memory” and the actions of countless anonymous lalas allow us to see how that structure was inhabited, negotiated, and sometimes pushed against in everyday life.
