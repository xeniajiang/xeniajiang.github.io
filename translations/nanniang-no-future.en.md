# Life Is Elsewhere?

## Nanniang, No Future, and Anthropology

### Nanniang

*Nanniang* (男娘) are often treated as a group best left unspoken. The term belongs to a cultural field adjacent to—but not identical with—English-language categories such as **femboy** and **cross-dresser**. Its first character, 男 (*nan*), means “male” or “man.” The second, 娘 (*niang*), is more difficult to pin down: depending on context, it can suggest femininity, a girl or young woman, or a mother. The compound therefore carries a mixture of gendered meanings that no single English label can fully reproduce.

Even within the “proper” language of identity respect, I find it surprisingly difficult to settle on an appropriate pronoun for *nanniang*. This is hardly accidental: the linguistic system that includes our norms of pronoun use was never built with them in mind. Language itself forms part of a regime of identity recognition. But what keeps happening in worlds where no such regime exists?

Let us begin with some real numbers. On Baidu Tieba, **女装吧** (*Nuzhuang Ba*) has 2.24 million followers and 8.19 million posts. Its name literally means the “women’s-clothing forum.” In this context, however, *nuzhuang* refers not merely to women’s apparel as a retail category, but to the practice of wearing clothing socially designated for women, especially among people assigned male.

**女装子吧** (*Nuzhuangzi Ba*) has 370,000 followers and 6.44 million posts. The term 女装子 entered Chinese internet culture from the Japanese 女装子, commonly romanized as *josoko*. It refers broadly to a person—usually someone assigned male—who wears feminine or women’s clothing. In Chinese online usage, however, the term has developed its own associations and does not map perfectly onto either *cross-dresser* or *femboy*.

These numbers remain substantial even after several related Tieba forums have been shut down. Popular *nanniang*-related videos recommended on Bilibili’s home page commonly receive between 10,000 and 100,000 views. On Xiaohongshu, many creators have been penalized by the platform simply for labeling themselves “male” while presenting in ways read as feminine.

Return to that figure of 2.24 million. It certainly includes people who want to escape the norms attached to their assigned gender, as well as people who are curious about them. Yet the act of following suggests, at the very least, that people assigned male who dress outside assigned-gender norms exert a real attraction and hold some significance in these followers’ lives.

“Someone who likes *nanniang*” also resembles a tacit identity of its own. The boundary between “people who like *nanniang*” and *nanniang* themselves is hardly clear, and movement between the two is common.

The shift from observer to participant is known as **翻车** (*fan che*)—literally, “overturning the vehicle” or “crashing.” The term still carries the residue of assigned-gender norms, framing participation as a mishap. Yet its very existence also reveals how weak the boundary has become: a special word is needed to describe movement across it.

We are thus confronted with a remarkably large number of people who harbor a desire to escape the dress norms attached to their assigned gender.

There are so many of them.

Imagine that a “little boy” grew up knowing that among people assigned male, there had always been a stable minority—perhaps only one to five percent—who wore dresses in everyday life, loved beauty, and expressed a feminine sensibility, and that this way of life could exist on its own terms, without obstruction. Many people might then have had childhoods with less confusion and more happiness.

Were such a world possible, this might simply be one category alongside muscular men, finance guys, and male athletes.

But under rigidly opposed gender norms, none of this has happened. For those who reject being trained into “proper men,” many bodily longings are converted into temporary, secret, unspeakable, and shameful acts. One thought appears again and again:

> If I keep going like this, I’m finished.

Talia Mae Bettcher’s work on trans phenomenology and multiple worlds offers an important route into lives that cannot be contained within a single regime of identity.

In “Trans Phenomenology,” Bettcher draws on María Lugones’s claim that multiple, interwoven worlds exist within relations of conflict, oppression, and resistance. Bettcher proposes that a trans person may be seen as one gender in a dominant world and as another in a world of resistance. This makes it possible to recognize one’s own multiplicity: to be one person in one world and another person in another.

The lives of many people regarded as *nanniang* have this character. Activities forced into hidden worlds, moments of shame entangled with desire, and the moments when one summons the energy to perform the gender role assigned to them all bear the mark of a phenomenology of multiple worlds.

Here, those worlds take concrete form. Moving from one world to another means moving between day and night, family life and Tieba, school or work and a rented room, a public profile and the shadow spaces created by algorithmic suppression.

## No Future

In queer theory, the most famous formulation of “no future” comes from Lee Edelman. In *No Future*, he criticizes reproductive futurism: politics is repeatedly organized around the Child as a symbol of the future, as though all present life must submit to a future that needs to be protected, continued, and passed on. Queerness is placed on the opposite side of this image of futurity.

I want to shift the problem of the future slightly. It appears not only through children, families, and reproduction, but also through a promise of recognition:

> Keep waiting, and one day your identity will be recognized. One day, your life will finally be given a place.

For many people who still care about queer lives and plural forms of existence, this is unquestionably a bleak time. At times, it feels as though the regime of identity recognition has jammed completely, to the point that merely avoiding further regression already counts as good fortune.

At the end of April, a courageous member of the community was labeled an “imperfect victim” by certain voices on Simplified Chinese social media simply for taking a supportive stance toward trans people. I doubt I was the only one who experienced the feeling of being encircled by multiple forces at once.

Those who need identity recognition are forced to stake their lives on waiting. They wait for the environment to improve and institutions to loosen. They hope that a safer way of living—one requiring less explanation—will eventually arrive.

Yet large-scale social change often unfolds over decades. How long do a person’s youth, prime of life, or middle age actually last? When more livable change fails to arrive, and when no sign suggests that it will arrive soon, waiting itself becomes a form of pressure.

That pressure comes both from the harshness of the present and from the expectations once invested in the future. The future had been imagined as compensation:

> The present can be endured because the future will be better.
>
> I can suppress myself for now because one day I will be recognized.

But when this expectation is blocked again and again, shame, anger, exhaustion, and self-doubt surge together.

In this essay, “no future” names such a moment: the future promised by the narrative of progress has failed to arrive on schedule, and one remains stranded in a present without promise.

To observe *nanniang* is to conduct a stress test on the narrative of progress. Their lives may contain little recognition of “gender identity,” but body images, fantasies of roles, and desires to shape the self do not disappear on that account.

From the perspective of trans communities, many *nanniang*, through appearance, interaction, and bodily practice, already inhabit forms of life commonly understood as feminine.

One hypothetical possibility is that, under a simpler and less discriminatory legal gender-recognition system, some of them might choose to change their documents and live as “ordinary women.” Yet in 2026, institutional mechanisms of gender recognition remain distributed across the world with extreme unevenness. For an ordinary person, relocating across regions or borders in pursuit of gender recognition is an almost impossible undertaking.

Trans communities sometimes put the matter this way:

> Nanniang are men; trans women are women.

This formulation has a protective function. It emphasizes respect for gender identity and upholds the legitimacy of first-person declarations of gender within the community.

Yet for the question pursued here, it is not enough.

We live precisely in a world that cannot adequately protect gender identity. Recognition and protection are themselves unevenly distributed resources for living, and they now appear to be growing scarcer. When identity cannot reliably offer a way out, life begins elsewhere, in hidden places.

It may not yet be nameable, but it is already real enough.

I do not intend to make this a purely pessimistic argument. In an earlier essay, I divided “gender” into eighteen smaller layers across three broad domains: social interaction, embodied experience, and assignment and recognition. These layers operate through certain assumptions of connection, which preserve the force of gender as an all-inclusive package of norms.

Seen this way, another implication embedded in *gender identity*—gender as sameness or self-identity—also becomes something we may need to question. We may carry countless fantasies of becoming a gender that is coherent, self-explanatory, and consistent everywhere.

Yet where external recognition is absent, dismantling the assumptions of connection among these layers—and moving carefully through multiple worlds—may itself offer a way of living through hostile conditions.

## Anthropology

At times, I feel that some orthodox forms of gender studies remain too invested in respectability and too deeply entangled with narratives of progress. As a result, *nanniang* are rarely approached with patience.

The phenomenon is quickly forced into a familiar set of questions: Is it misogynistic? Does it reproduce binary stereotypes? Does it resist assigned gender? Is it merely an artifact of platform algorithms?

The questions arrive too quickly, and life itself is pushed aside.

Yet the vlogs that place the character 男, “male,” in their titles and cover images while staging a feminine manner before the camera possess a texture close to lived reality. They do not rush to resolve their contradictions, nor do they always offer a clear declaration of identity.

A person may retain traces of assigned gender in a title while entering another world through body, clothing, and camera. This lived experience, marked as “inconsistent,” has not been neatly resolved by theory. Yet it already constitutes a way of life.

I see here a field especially suited to cultural anthropology. This kind of observation also seems to offer me a certain insight into how one might approach life.

Institutions always support only some lives. Those whose lives remain unsupported, or who cannot wait for support to arrive, retain—simply by virtue of being human—the capacity to arrange their lives strategically.

Institutions are rigid; human lives can be remarkably supple.

This thought may find echoes across many eras and among many marginalized groups. The institutional gap remains, and critique still has its place. But we have lived inside promises of progress for so long that when those promises are withdrawn, we experience a profound loss of gravity.

How, then, do people inhabit a flawed world?

This may be the more difficult question that plural forms of life pose to our time.

Life does not stop after the loss of gravity. It merely withdraws from the grand future into smaller places, into a temporary resting place called the present.

A person remains embedded here and now, sometimes feeling stuck, yet unable simply to fly away. We are always thrown into a particular time and place.

Perhaps, in another time and another place, I would be recognized and could live another life. But if, along this timeline, I remain obstructed at every turn, then this only shows that I am an ordinary human being bound to time and place—just one small person, neither a citizen of the world nor a traveler through time.

Perhaps this is the most anthropologically luminous part of the entire question.
