# No Longer Hu(Wo)man

## I’m Sorry for Being Trans

This is the voice of a trans person, about the contradictions, pain, and absurdity that cannot be fully captured by theory on the path toward becoming oneself. Perhaps you, too, will see something of yourself in it. A brief, not very “academic” essay that can be read quickly—a piece of “trans satire.”

Trigger Warning: transphobic rhetoric, “TERFs,” and “political lesbians.”

I’m sorry for being trans. For so many days, I worshipped gender as a god and gently spat on the corpse of the seemingly obsolete category of sex, even though, in the eyes of the world, that corpse still shone brilliantly; yet somehow I forgot that I had never truly escaped my awareness of the absurdity of the gender system. This absurdity became entangled with my attachment to norms. I was accused of reproducing an oppressive gender system, while no one cared about the oppressor; gradually, I no longer dared to show the slightest disrespect toward the god called gender, afraid that even the smallest act of blasphemy would be seized upon and used to strip away the female identity on which my survival depended—worship and fear closed in around me.

Yet the gender system remains riddled with holes, just like gender norms themselves: diffuse and impossible to grasp. This makes trans people’s attachment to norms resemble Sisyphus and his boulder, or the title of a musical: *Next to Normal*.

The musical *Next to Normal* depicts the female protagonist’s difficult struggle with bipolar disorder.

Consider these ceaseless collisions of paradox:

A person can never want to become what they “already are.”

A queer context that celebrates the absence of all bodily norms in fact encompasses no concrete embodied experience. It appears endlessly inclusive, yet is empty.

I know that I am performing gender, and yet I am somehow willing to perform it only in one particular way. Who can tell me why I cannot simply perform it differently? You say it is because structures punish deviation? Then what happens when the punishments attached to the “two” available scripts are not very different, or when crossing gender boundaries is punished far more severely? What, exactly, is pointing me in one direction rather than another? I know that everyone performs gender, so why are people only concerned with whether my performance is insufficient or excessive?

If my identity is thoroughly queer, why can I not take pride in the “deviant” act of entering a men’s restroom as a woman? Is it only because I am a pathetic remnant of patriarchy, a blinded conformist trying to please the system? When queer theory awakens a queer sense of self in me and helps dissolve part of the trans impulse to seek normativity, how am I supposed to regard those who remain in pain and do everything they can to change the appearance of their bodies? How am I to regard the bodily memories that have been quietly bracketed out? When I call myself queer, am I also quietly placing an elitist halo over my head? Why must the convergence of trans politics and feminism be built on degrading some trans people as obedient echoes of gender discipline?

How “fortunate” I am to be a woman who has been “successfully socialized.” Look at the fucking body society gave me, a body that meets the “standards” of womanhood. Yes, I am a fucking woman. My body is finite. I am not a cyborg queer living in a void. It is not easy to live in the 2020s, and no one who claims to know what female experience is can deny it. People who look like me are prepared, simply because of the path by which I arrived here and the structure of my desire, to expose my body and my life to the front line of bodily violation; in the familiar hot-air-balloon thought experiment, it is easy to decide that one person is unworthy of life and should be thrown overboard, as long as enough people want me gone. Yet violence should be named as violence, not presented as the defense of some ideology.

I admire Andrea Long Chu’s insight. She gently sets aside the violence and disgust that TERFs package as political opinion and instead adopts an anthropological perspective, tracing their countless connections to baiting and trolling culture. A woman with a secure sense of her own worth would not seriously engage with the misogynistic posts on the Sun Xiaochuan Tieba forum, much less offer them a scholarly response. The fact that “TERF” can already function as a collective name in polemical discourse is itself a discursive victory among allies: who cares about “gender-critical ideology”? It is like packaging certain aggressive men as “incels”; once they have been packaged and named, there is no longer any need to take them seriously.

Interestingly, responding and defending oneself becomes an impulse. From the first time someone asks, “Why do you think you are a woman?” or “Why are you trans?”, we learn to protect ourselves with theory and rhetoric. Yet other people cannot understand bodies and desire; they can only understand the “wrong body,” a cheap, flattened fairy-tale narrative, no better than *The Frog Prince*. Eventually, even when dealing with groups such as TERFs—people you already know will distort everything you say and argue in bad faith—you cannot help feeling that a response is necessary. Debating a TERF is like being an adult forced to argue with your boss’s obnoxious little child: you cite books and evidence, while the child periodically screams for a parent to intervene, and so you continue witnessing the absurdity of the world. Or it is like being a bullied middle-school student who cannot stop solemnly explaining that “bullying is wrong”; the more seriously you say it, the more you excite the delinquent bully, until you become trapped in an endless cycle of serving as someone else’s emotional outlet.

When they need you, you have always been one of them; when they despise you, you never were. A British trans woman mathematician receives a prestigious mathematical honor, and women immediately appear to say that this proves women face no inherent disadvantage in mathematics, that everything is only discourse and power; men reply, oh, but she is trans, not cis. As a trans person and a gender studies student—even as a graduate of an “elite university”—I can easily be placed in the category of those who are useful and receive a little red flower called “gender affirmation.” But what about trans people who cannot overcome difficulties several times greater than those faced by cis people, and who, surrounded by family conflict, academic pressure, poor health, employment discrimination, and emotional isolation, become merely “ordinary”? What about trans people who, like fat people, people with polycystic ovary syndrome, neurodivergent people, and disabled people, provoke “physical disgust” in supposedly flawless and normal cisgender people? Should the social Darwinists who have seeped into every pore of society be allowed to push them into the hell reserved for the Other?

Some people who call themselves feminists somehow refuse to believe in trans lesbians. While many cis women occasionally joke that they wish they were men, trans lesbians enter the world with the premise that “male privilege is available as long as they remain hidden,” yet still leap without hesitation into countless punishments for crossing gender boundaries. Both the self they seek to become and the partners toward whom their desire is directed are women. What could demonstrate greater devotion than abandoning a supposedly “low-cost male identity”? Are these not the pure women once desired by political lesbianism? Even bodily desire forms part of this unity. Compared with political lesbians whose desires remain heterosexual, we possess two additional utopian qualities: first, the desire we project outward is directed toward women; second, we remain perpetually attached to the experience of being women, patiently, and without letting ourselves off easily, shaping the selves we encounter in the mirror. Is this not more utopian than radical feminists who remain unable to escape “bad sex”? I have no intention of reproducing moral judgments about desire, bodies, or sexual orientation; I am merely extending that discourse slightly in order to bring its tensions into view. I acknowledge that desire can never be completely mastered by politics, and even one’s own body frequently escapes the control of reason. I will not return to the old path of separating mind from body.

Bodies, experience, writing, mutual love. Give me a pen or a microphone, and I will simply write or speak for myself. Experience is not up for debate; yet when empathy arrives, it speaks for itself.
