# The Imagined Sour Lemon

## Trans-Exclusionary Discourse, Accusations of “Hyper-Femininity,” and the Logic of the Market for Lemons

Trans women are frequently mocked for “reinforcing stereotypes” or being “hyper-feminine.” Some self-proclaimed progressives use this as a reason to regard trans women’s gender expression as the product of conservatism and backwardness, thereby finding a convenient excuse for trans-exclusionary discourse. A common line of argument goes like this: dresses, high heels, gentleness, and submissiveness do not define womanhood; anyone who imagines these things as symbols of women is not a “real” woman. By introducing economic theories of signaling under information asymmetry, this article explains why, in many circumstances, trans women’s gender performances are in fact rational strategies—and sometimes the only strategies available to help them obtain the safest environment possible. This article is written to remain as intuitive as possible. At approximately 3,500 Chinese characters, it takes around ten minutes to read.

Note: This article focuses specifically on trans women. Transgender men and nonbinary people face both similar and distinctive circumstances, and their experiences should not be treated as interchangeable.

## The Market for Lemons, Signaling, and the Function of Examinations

The theory of the “market for lemons” originated in George Akerlof’s theoretical contribution of 1970, for which he later received the 2001 Nobel Prize in Economics. The theory introduces the following kind of market:

(1) There are two kinds of lemons: sweet lemons, worth 10 yuan, and sour lemons, worth 5 yuan. There is a group of buyers and a group of sellers. Sellers know whether the lemons they are selling are sweet or sour, but buyers possess no such information and can only make a rough inference about quality from the price set by the seller.

(2) Because buyers lack information, they are unwilling to make a high offer. They instead tend to offer a price somewhere between the value of a sweet lemon and that of a sour one—6 yuan, for example—because they would suffer a substantial loss if they paid too much and ended up buying a sour lemon.

(3) The market price therefore stabilizes below 10 yuan, which means that sellers supplying sweet lemons are destined to incur losses. Under rational conditions, sellers of sweet lemons will gradually withdraw from the market, leaving only sour lemons behind.

The mechanism of this market demonstrates that under conditions of information asymmetry, high-quality participants cannot be identified at a reasonable price and are instead forced to exit the market. Lower-quality participants “survive” because they are better able to adapt to an environment of low trust and low offers. This is known as **adverse selection**. When buyers lower their offers in order to avoid risk, the system systematically penalizes high-quality participants who have no credible way to distinguish themselves from lower-quality ones. This produces a structural consequence: when signals cannot effectively distinguish quality, rational choices lead to a decline in overall quality.

In economics, the key to solving the market-for-lemons problem lies in introducing a signaling mechanism. If there is a signal whose cost structure is asymmetric—one that is easier, or more worthwhile, for high-quality participants to bear, but difficult for lower-quality participants to imitate—the market may once again become differentiated.

The most typical example is the signaling and screening function of examinations within the educational system. Education is precisely such a signal. The key question is not whether the course content itself directly increases productivity, but that completing a long-term and systematic education imposes different costs on different people. People with weaker learning abilities must expend more effort, while those who are less capable of making long-term investments or tolerating delayed returns face a higher opportunity cost of studying. This is precisely why educational credentials can function as a useful screening device in a labor market characterized by information asymmetry. The signaling mechanism works roughly as follows:

(1) An employer offers a position that generates a positive return worth 50,000 yuan. Two kinds of candidates apply: people with strong learning ability, Type A, and people with weak learning ability, Type B. The employer hopes to select Type A candidates for the job.

(2) The employer establishes an examination. Each course imposes a cost equivalent to 10,000 yuan on Type A candidates. Economics often quantifies pleasure and pain as “utility,” which can sometimes be expressed in monetary terms. Each course imposes a cost equivalent to 30,000 yuan on Type B candidates. The employer establishes a threshold: anyone who completes a certain number of courses receives an employee certificate and may take the job. How many courses should be required in order to ensure that all successful candidates are Type A?

(3) One course is insufficient because both Type A and Type B candidates still receive a positive net return. With two courses, Type A candidates incur a cost of 20,000 yuan, while Type B candidates incur a cost of 60,000 yuan. Type B candidates will voluntarily withdraw, making the mechanism effective. The mechanism continues to screen successfully up to a threshold of four courses. An examination requirement of two to four courses therefore becomes a signaling and screening mechanism. In most cases, postgraduate entrance examinations, civil-service examinations, and professional-certification examinations operate according to the same logic.

## The Imagined Sour Lemon

Mary Douglas was an important figure in British social anthropology whose work examined how systems of classification, symbolic orders, and social norms shape people’s understanding of the world. In *Purity and Danger*, she proposed that “dirt is not a material property, but the result of order being disrupted.” Her work profoundly influenced discussions of norms and transgression in anthropology, sociology, and cultural studies.

In *Purity and Danger*, anthropologist Mary Douglas revealed that cultures often classify things that resist easy categorization as “dirty.” Groups that fall outside dominant classificatory models invariably become attached to the most stubborn forms of stigma. At birth, medical discourse performs a binary assignment called “sex” upon every individual, thereby producing what is called “assigned sex/gender at birth.” Within society’s binary framework of gender expression, people’s understanding of sex becomes subject to various essentialist inferences and cultural constructions. Yet through the circulation of discourse, this interpretation of sex is gradually naturalized and even called “biological,” precisely because its constructed dimension has been obscured to the greatest possible extent. For example, as someone who was previously assigned male, I was once asked by a friend whether my hair grew more slowly than that of people assigned female. This is an example of how the rigid binary gender norm of “women have long hair, men have short hair”—a norm that emerged from modern and contemporary white middle-class culture—produces an essentialist fantasy about sex: that women’s hair “biologically” grows faster.

The assignment of sex thus becomes one of the most effective forms of classificatory power in the world. This power of classification is implanted in what the public calls “common sense,” making every way of life that does not conform to one’s assignment appear “incomprehensible.” Mainstream society casts trans women as “boundary-crossers”—in reality, as people who have trespassed against the normative boundaries of classificatory power, rather than against any objectively existing boundary—and, through media and algorithms, continually reinforces a series of demonizing fantasies: invaders of women’s spaces, ugly monsters, people who “lost their way” out of curiosity, bewitched victims waiting to be rescued, and the mentally ill. Within the discourse of cultural stigma, the “sour lemon” invoked again and again is often nothing more than an imagined fabrication—a phantom constructed through discourse with no corresponding figure in reality.

“Transition” and medical “gatekeeping” are closely related to signaling and screening mechanisms. “Mainstream” society may defend itself in the following way: because we cannot distinguish between “real trans people” and “fake trans people,” we are like buyers who cannot tell whether a lemon is sweet or sour. To avoid risk, we have no choice but to lower our offer—the offer here being understood as the degree of gender affirmation or the friendliness extended in social interaction. The transitional “asceticism” demanded of trans women by society—diagnostic certificates, long-term hormone replacement, years of “observation and follow-up” as thresholds for surgery, and painful, high-risk operations—functions, to some extent, like the ever-expanding number of examination subjects imposed in order to screen employees. Regrettably, “whether one looks like a woman” has also absurdly become a central part of this signaling and screening mechanism. The evaluative system assumes that imagined “sour lemons” will be unable or unwilling to meet these ascetic thresholds and therefore demands, through social transition and medical gatekeeping, that trans women prove themselves to be “pure” and “authentic.” But what if we point out, sharply, that this imagined sour lemon is itself a product of classificatory power? What if it never truly existed in the first place?

“Witch hunts” refer to the social campaigns of early modern Europe, approximately from the fifteenth to the seventeenth century, in which people identified as “witches” were systematically accused, tried, and executed amid the interaction of religion, law, and popular panic. Their targets were mostly women, marginalized people, and those who deviated from established norms. The imagined “sour lemon” of trans-exclusionary discourse constitutes a witch hunt for a new era.

## Multiple Examiners and the Fallback Performance Mechanism

Let us assume that trans women are “candidates being screened.” Mainstream society becomes the authority that issues certificates of “womanhood,” while the ascetic demands of transition and the performance of femininity resemble the subjects required in an examination. When trans women walk down the street or post about their daily lives online, they often encounter multiple “examiners” of gender expression whose standards differ greatly from one another. We may therefore construct the following model—though we will later return to criticize it, because the very existence of such a model is already absurd:

(1) Some “progressives” appear to impose relatively low assessment standards. They do not judge trans women according to whether they conform to stereotypical forms of femininity when deciding whether to extend gender affirmation, such as using the correct pronouns or sharing spaces. Yet out of a certain naïve loyalty to progressive discourse, they arrogantly mock trans women as “hyper-feminine” whenever they use a greater number of traditional feminine symbols to express themselves. This form of affirmation is unstable and may be withdrawn.

(2) Some “conservatives” impose much higher standards. They evaluate trans women according to rigid stereotypes. If a trans woman’s gender expression deviates in the slightest from their understanding of traditional femininity, they unhesitatingly inflict the violence of misgendering. A trans woman can therefore avoid violence and obtain their affirmation only by appearing sufficiently “feminine” in every respect. The “experts” responsible for medical gatekeeping reproduce the same conservative judgments grounded in stereotypes—for example, absurd practices such as refusing to accept that trans women may be attracted to women while making exceptions for trans women who wear Japanese schoolgirl uniforms.

(3) The survival goal of trans women is to encounter less violence and receive more affirmation. Violence is distributed unevenly and asymmetrically. In most circumstances, the violence that conservatives are capable of mobilizing is more frightening. A seemingly rational form of gender expression therefore emerges: trans women are compelled to engage in a kind of **fallback performance**. Only by satisfying the highest demands of conservatives—by becoming a woman who is extremely feminine according to stereotypical expectations—can they minimize the number of situations in which they must endure the pain of being misgendered. The result of this decision immediately becomes the object of disgust and mockery from so-called progressives.

## Collusion and Monopoly in the Cisgender Market of Evaluation

Within an order constructed through classificatory power, cisgender people need only present identification documents in order to receive affirmation of their identities. They therefore rarely become “candidates” whose gender identities are subjected to assessment. This entire “market of evaluation,” jointly constituted by doctors, family members, strangers, the media, and others, functions in practice as a buyer-side monopoly over gender identity. Cisgender people become the monopolistic evaluators, while trans women are forced to become complete **price takers**, with no bargaining power whatsoever. They can only adjust their behavior as best they can in response to the “price” offered by social culture, hoping to obtain a small corner in which they may breathe. It must be emphasized that the formation of this market is itself violent, absurd, and unjust. It instead raises the “burden of proof” for everyone, including cisgender people, and hardens the binary structure. When cisgender women are required online to prove that they are “not trans,” is this not precisely what conservatives desire? Cisgender women, too, have ultimately failed to escape judgment within the binary framework.

Some people call themselves “progressives,” yet because they do not understand the mechanism of this signaling market, they casually offer criticism from a position of privilege under the banner of naïve progressive aspirations. But are trans women really the ones who reinforce binary stereotypes? The forces that do so are the stigmatizing fantasies that continually circulate through culture; the refusal to extend understanding and trust without even pausing to think; the entrenched positions of power monopolized by conservatives; and the firm control exercised by conservative forces over critical points in trans women’s lives, such as the diagnostic authority of so-called medical experts. Together, these forces construct the monopolistic wall of the evaluation market. They are the real agents that solidify the binary framework. Merely pointing out that trans women are scapegoats is insufficient. Only by following the evaluative mechanisms of this “market” that turn trans women into scapegoats, and directing attention toward those who actually reinforce the entire system, can we finally point at the elephant in the room.

Note: Trans women’s performances of gender expression do not arise solely from the need to survive. An economic simplification may partially obscure trans women’s subjectivity. Any outward presentation may be motivated entirely by self-pleasure or simply by “because I want to.” The model in this article serves only to foreground the difficulty of the environment in which trans women survive and to analyze meso- and macro-level mechanisms from the perspective of social structure.
